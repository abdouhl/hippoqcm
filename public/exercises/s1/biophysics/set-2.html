Exercise 1 — Force needed to burst a red blood cell sheet

Calculate the force required to burst a red blood cell, knowing that isotonicity corresponds to 9 g/L of NaCl and complete hemolysis to 3 g/L of NaCl. The erythrocyte is modelled as a cylinder of diameter d = 7 µm and thickness e = 1 µm. Its membrane is assumed to be semi-permeable.

  • 9 g/L NaCl (isotonic = inside the cell)
  • 3 g/L NaCl (hemolysis)
  • d = 7 µm, e = 1 µm
  • MNaCl = 58.5 g/mol, α = 1
  • T = 37 °C = 310 K
Hint

The cell's content is isotonic to 9 g/L NaCl. In 3 g/L the outside is hypotonic: water rushes in, and the membrane bursts under the osmotic pressure difference between the two. Force = pressure × surface of the cell.

Solution
  1. Law Osmolarity p. 1
    ω = 2C (NaCl, α = 1, β = 2)
    inside: ω1 = 2 × 9 / 58.5 = 0.3077 osmol/L
    outside at hemolysis: ω2 = 2 × 3 / 58.5 = 0.1026 osmol/L
    Δω = 0.2051 osmol/L = 205.1 osmol/m³
  2. Law Osmotic pressure between two solutions p. 3
    Δπ = (ω1 − ω2) R T
    Δπ = 205.1 × 8.31 × 310 = 5.28 × 10⁵ Pa

  3. Surface of the cylinder (two discs + the side):

    S = 2 × π(d/2)² + π d e = 2π(3.5 × 10⁻⁶)² + π × 7 × 10⁻⁶ × 1 × 10⁻⁶
    S = 7.70 × 10⁻¹¹ + 2.20 × 10⁻¹¹ = 9.90 × 10⁻¹¹ m²
  4. F = Δπ × S = 5.28 × 10⁵ × 9.90 × 10⁻¹¹ = 5.23 × 10⁻⁵ N
Δπ ≈ 5.29 × 10⁵ Pa · F ≈ 5.23 × 10⁻⁵ N

Same as the sheet's answer (5.29 × 10⁵ Pa comes out with R = 8.314; with R = 8.31 you get 5.28 × 10⁵ — both fine). The key uses the cylinder's total surface.

The sheet doesn't state the temperature: body temperature, 37 °C = 310 K, is assumed (it reproduces the key). F = P × S is the definition of pressure, not something these lessons teach.
Traps
  • Using one concentration (π = ωRT with 9 g/L): what matters is the difference between inside and outside.
  • ω × 1000 (osmol/L → osmol/m³) and T in K, or the pressure is off by 1000.
  • µm² → m²: (10⁻⁶)² = 10⁻¹².

Exercise 2 — Hemodialysis: the artificial kidney sheet

An artificial kidney used in chronic hemodialysis has a diffusion membrane (Cuprophan) of surface S = 1 m². Across it, the patient's blood on one side and a constantly renewed fluid (dialysate) — isotonic to plasma and free of urea — on the other side are brought into equilibrium. We consider only urea elimination. Recall that water and urea diffuse relatively rapidly across cell membranes.

  1. Explain why: a) the renewed fluid must be isotonic to plasma; b) not only the blood but all the water of the body, of volume V = 40 L, is thereby cleared of urea.
  2. Towards what value should the blood urea concentration c(t) tend after a very long hemodialysis? For what physiological reason does it not do so?
  3. Assume the urea concentration gradient is uniform across the membrane's thickness, and its permeability to urea is P = 1.5 × 10⁻⁶ m/s. If the initial uremia is C0 = 2 g/L, calculate the initial mass flow rate of urea.
  4. If the daily production of urea is 25 g/day, what will the patient's uremia be after a very long time?
  • S = 1 m²
  • V = 40 L
  • P = 1.5 × 10⁻⁶ m/s
  • C0 = 2 g/L = 2 kg/m³
  • production 25 g/day
  • dialysate: urea = 0
Hint

1) Think of what an osmotic pressure difference would do to the blood's water. 2) The dialysate is renewed, so its urea stays at 0. 3) Fick with a uniform gradient: dC/dx = ΔC / l. 4) "After a very long time" = steady state: urea removed per second = urea produced per second.

Solution

1) a) Why isotonic to plasma?

Law Tonicity p. 4
isotonic ⇔ π1 = π2 ⇔ no water flow

Water moves toward the higher osmolarity. A hypotonic dialysate would push water into the blood (and then into the red cells → hemolysis); a hypertonic one would dehydrate the patient. Isotonic: no water exchange — only urea (absent from the dialysate) diffuses out.

1) b) Why all 40 L of body water?

As blood loses urea, a concentration difference appears between blood and the interstitial and intracellular water. Urea (and water) cross cell and capillary membranes quickly, so urea keeps diffusing from the tissues into the blood, which carries it to the dialyser. All the body water behaves as one compartment of 40 L that is cleared together.

2) Limit of c(t)

Law Diffusion until equal concentrations p. 3
C∞ = (C1iV1 + C2iV2) / (V1 + V2)

The dialysate is constantly renewed (its volume is in effect infinite, with urea = 0), so equal concentrations means c(t) → 0. It doesn't get there because the body (the liver) keeps producing urea (25 g/day, see 4): c(t) settles where elimination balances production.

c(t) → 0 in theory; not reached because urea is produced continuously

3) Initial mass flow rate

  1. Law Fick's law with mass concentration p. 4
    Q = dm/dt = −D S′ dCp/dx
    uniform gradient: Qi = D S′l |ΔCi|

    The permeability groups the diffusion coefficient and the thickness: P = D / l (m²/s ÷ m = m/s). Taking S′ = S:

    Qi = P · S · |ΔCi| = P · S · C0
  2. Qi = 1.5 × 10⁻⁶ × 1 × 2 = 3 × 10⁻⁶ kg/s
    = 3 mg/s ≈ 10.8 g/h
Qi = 3 × 10⁻⁶ kg/s = 3 mg/s (≈ 10.8 g/h)
Permeability P = D/l isn't defined in your lessons (they stop at Fick's law with D, S′ and l) — it's the standard definition, from the lesson's own Qi = (DS′/l)|ΔCi|. The pore area S′ is taken equal to the membrane surface S, as the sheet gives only S.

4) Uremia after a very long time

  1. Steady state: urea eliminated per second = urea produced per second, with the same law at concentration C∞:

    production = 25 × 10⁻³ kg / 86 400 s = 2.89 × 10⁻⁷ kg/s
    P · S · C∞ = 2.89 × 10⁻⁷ → C∞ = 2.89 × 10⁻⁷ / (1.5 × 10⁻⁶ × 1) = 0.193 kg/m³
C∞ ≈ 0.19 g/L
This assumes the dialysis runs continuously (the sheet's "very long time"); the sheet gives no answer for this exercise.
Traps
  • g/L = kg/m³ — no conversion needed, but L ≠ m³ for volumes.
  • 25 g/day must be turned into kg/s (÷ 86 400) before comparing with Q.
  • Thinking dialysis brings uremia to 0: production never stops.

Exercise 3 — Peritoneal dialysis sheet

A patient arrives at the emergency department in septic shock with anuria. To compensate for the renal failure, a peritoneal dialysis session is decided: a volume Ve of a solution isotonic to plasma but free of urea is injected into the peritoneal cavity. Across the barrier formed by the capillary walls and the peritoneum, a diffusion equilibrium is established over a surface S between the blood of the peritoneal vessels and the injected fluid.

  1. Explain why the volumes of the two compartments (internal and external) don't change during dialysis.
  2. If C0 is the initial uremia, what will the uremia C∞ be at equilibrium? The body contains Vi = 40 L, and Ve = 2 L is injected.
  3. After equilibrium, the fluid is aspirated and a second cycle is started, and so on. How many cycles are needed to bring the uremia from C0 = 1.2 g/L to the near-normal Cn = 0.4 g/L?
  4. Each cycle lasts τ = 20 min, and diffusion equilibrium is reached by its end. Calculate: a) the total duration of dialysis to reach 0.4 g/L; b) the urea clearance — the virtual volume of blood completely cleared of its urea per unit of time — in mL/min.
  • Vi = 40 L
  • Ve = 2 L, urea 0, isotonic
  • C0 = 1.2 g/L → Cn = 0.4 g/L
  • τ = 20 min per cycle
Hint

Each cycle is a diffusion to equal concentrations between 40 L (at the current uremia) and 2 L (at 0): the uremia is multiplied by the same factor every cycle. Solve C0 × factorn ≤ Cn with logarithms.

Solution

1) Volumes don't change

Law Tonicity p. 4
isotonic ⇔ π1 = π2 ⇔ no water flow

The injected fluid is isotonic to plasma: no osmotic pressure difference, so no net water flow. Urea leaves the blood, but it diffuses freely across the barrier and isn't osmotically active, so its movement doesn't drag water either. Both volumes stay the same.

2) Uremia at equilibrium

  1. Law Diffusion until equal concentrations p. 3
    C∞ = C1iV1 + C2iV2V1 + V2
    C∞ = (C0 × 40 + 0 × 2) / (40 + 2) = (40/42) C0 = 0.952 C0
C∞ = C0 · Vi/(Vi + Ve) = 0.952 C0

3) Number of cycles

  1. Each new cycle starts from the previous equilibrium with fresh urea-free fluid, so every cycle multiplies the uremia by 40/42:

    Cn = C0 × (40/42)n
  2. (42/40)n = C0/Cn = 1.2/0.4 = 3
    n = ln 3 / ln 1.05 = 1.0986 / 0.04879 = 22.5

  3. n must be a whole number of cycles and the uremia must reach 0.4 g/L: after 22 cycles it is still 0.410 g/L, after 23 it is 0.391 g/L.

n = 23 cycles

4) a) Total duration

t = 23 × 20 = 460 min
t = 460 min ≈ 7 h 40 min

4) b) Urea clearance

At the end of each cycle the 2 L of aspirated fluid contain urea at C∞, the blood's concentration at that moment: the urea removed is exactly what Ve = 2 L of blood would contain. So every 20 min, 2 L of blood is virtually cleared:

clearance = Ve / τ = 2000 mL / 20 min = 100 mL/min
Clearance = 100 mL/min
"Clearance" isn't defined in your lessons — the sheet defines it. Referred to the uremia at the start of each cycle instead, the cleared volume is 2 × 40/42 = 1.90 L per cycle, i.e. 95 mL/min; 100 mL/min is the usual answer. The sheet gives no answer for this exercise.
Traps
  • Rounding n down to 22: the uremia would still be above 0.4 g/L.
  • Subtracting the same amount each cycle: it's the same factor (40/42), so the amount removed shrinks every cycle.
  • Using Vi + Ve = 42 L as the body's volume after injection: the fluid is aspirated at each cycle.
