Exercise 1 — Saturating vapor pressure sheet

  1. What is the minimum mass of water (g) that must be introduced into a 5-litre chamber at 37 °C to obtain saturating water vapor?
  2. The 5-litre chamber is compressed to a volume of 3 litres, and 0.219 g of water is then introduced into it. What would the vapor pressure be?
  3. Same question as 2), but with a final chamber volume of V = 10 L.
  • Psat(37 °C) = 6.27 kPa
  • T = 37 + 273 = 310 K
  • Mwater = 18 g/mol
  • R = 8.31
Hint

The most water the chamber can hold as vapor is the amount that brings the vapor to Psat: nmax = PsatV / RT. Then compare the water you put in with nmax for each volume.

Solution

1) Minimum mass for saturation (V = 5 L)

  1. Law Ideal gas, at saturation p. 2
    nmax = Psat · VR T
    nmax = 6270 × 5 × 10⁻³ / (8.31 × 310) = 1.217 × 10⁻² mol
  2. m = n × M = 1.217 × 10⁻² × 18 = 0.219 g
mmin ≈ 0.219 g

2) 0.219 g in V = 3 L

  1. nmax(3 L) = 6270 × 3 × 10⁻³ / (8.31 × 310) = 7.30 × 10⁻³ mol = 0.131 g

  2. We have 0.219 g > 0.131 g → the chamber is saturated: only 0.131 g is vapor, about 0.088 g stays liquid. The vapor pressure is then Psat, which depends only on temperature.

Pvapor = Psat = 6.27 kPa

3) 0.219 g in V = 10 L

  1. nmax(10 L) = 6270 × 10 × 10⁻³ / (8.31 × 310) = 2.43 × 10⁻² mol = 0.438 g

  2. 0.219 g < 0.438 g → everything evaporates and the vapor is not saturated: P = nRT/V.

    n = 0.219 / 18 = 1.217 × 10⁻² mol
    P = 1.217 × 10⁻² × 8.31 × 310 / (10 × 10⁻³) = 3134 Pa
Pvapor ≈ 3.13 kPa (half of Psat: same water, double volume)
Traps
  • V in m³ (L × 10⁻³), T in K, P in Pa — R = 8.31 is SI.
  • Using Psat in 3): below nmax the vapor pressure is nRT/V, smaller than Psat.
  • In 2) Pvapor doesn't rise above Psat: the extra water just stays liquid.

Exercise 2 — Partition of a solute between two immiscible liquids sheet

At temperature T, a solute A is dissolved in two immiscible liquids (1) and (2). μ10 and μ20 are the standard chemical potentials of A in solvent (1) and in solvent (2). Both solutions are ideal.

  1. At equilibrium, what is the relationship between the chemical potential μ1 of A in (1) and μ2 of A in (2)?
  2. Deduce that at equilibrium the concentrations C1 and C2 of A in (1) and (2) satisfy C2/C1 = k, where k is a constant to determine that depends only on temperature.
  3. 1 L of water (solvent 1) and 1 L of benzene (solvent 2) are poured into a container, then 1 mmol of phenol (solute A) is added; it dissolves in both. Find C1 and C2.
  • T = 298 K
  • μ10 − μ20 = −898 J/mol
  • V1 = V2 = 1 L
  • n = 1 mmol
Hint

Two phases at equilibrium have equal chemical potentials. Write μ = μ⁰ + RT ln a in each phase with a = C/C0 (ideal), and isolate ln(C2/C1). For 3), add the conservation of the 1 mmol.

Solution

1) Equilibrium condition

  1. Law Two phases at equilibrium p. 4
    equal temperatures, pressures and chemical potentials
μ1 = μ2

2) The partition constant k

  1. Law Chemical potential + activity of an ideal solution (γ = 1) p. 4 p. 2
    μi = μi0 + RT ln(Ci/C0)
    μ10 + RT ln(C1/C0) = μ20 + RT ln(C2/C0)
    RT ln(C2/C1) = μ10 − μ20
  2. C2C1 = k = expμ10 − μ20R T

    μ0 depends only on T, so k depends only on T.

k = exp[(μ10 − μ20)/RT]

3) Phenol between water and benzene

  1. (μ10 − μ20)/RT = −898 / (8.31 × 298) = −0.363
    k = e−0.363 = 0.696 ≈ 0.7

  2. The 1 mmol is shared, and both volumes are 1 L: C1 × 1 + C2 × 1 = 1 mmol, with C2 = k C1.

    C1 = 1 / (1 + k) = 1 / 1.696 = 0.590 mmol/L
    C2 = k C1 = 0.696 × 0.590 = 0.410 mmol/L
k ≈ 0.7 · C1 (water) ≈ 0.59 mmol/L · C2 (benzene) ≈ 0.41 mmol/L

Same as the sheet's answer (0.7; 0.59; 0.41). μ0 is lower in water (μ10 − μ20 < 0), so phenol prefers water: C1 > C2.

Traps
  • Sign: k = e(μ₁⁰ − μ₂⁰)/RT gives C2/C1, not C1/C2 — inverting it swaps the two answers.
  • Equilibrium means equal μ, not equal μ0 or equal concentrations.

Exercise 3 — Osmolarity of a mixture sheet

1 litre of solution contains:

  • 10 mL of HCl at 1 mol/L
  • 7.5 mL of H2SO4 at 2 mol/L
  • 5.55 g of CaCl2 (M = 111 g/mol)
  • 14.4 g of glucose (M = 180 g/mol)

The electrolytes are fully dissociated. Calculate the osmolarity of the solution.

  • Vsolution = 1 L
  • α = 1 for the electrolytes
Hint

Moles of each solute (stock volume × concentration, or mass / M), divide by 1 L, count the ions β of each, then add the ωi.

Solution
  1. Law Osmolarity of several solutes p. 2
    ω = Σ [1 + αi(βi − 1)] Ci = Σ βi Ci (α = 1)
  2. SoluteC (mol/L)βω (osmol/L)
    HCl → H⁺ + Cl⁻10 × 10⁻³ × 1 = 0.01020.020
    H2SO4 → 2H⁺ + SO42−7.5 × 10⁻³ × 2 = 0.01530.045
    CaCl2 → Ca2+ + 2Cl⁻5.55 / 111 = 0.05030.150
    Glucose (non-electrolyte)14.4 / 180 = 0.08010.080
  3. ω = 0.020 + 0.045 + 0.150 + 0.080 = 0.295 osmol/L
ω = 0.295 osmol/L = 295 mosmol/L

Same as the sheet's answer — and close to plasma (≈ 300 mosmol/L).

Traps
  • β of H2SO4 and CaCl2 is 3, not 2.
  • Volumes of stock acid in mL: n = V(L) × C.
  • Glucose doesn't dissociate: ω = C.

Exercise 4 — Osmolarity of urine from its freezing point sheet

In one litre of urine with a freezing-point depression of −1.4 °C, 11.7 g of NaCl and 15 g of urea (M = 60 g/mol) were found.

  1. Calculate the total osmolarity, then those of NaCl and of urea.
  2. What can we conclude? Can this excess osmolarity be attributed to albumin (Mal = 70 000 g/mol)? What other substance should be looked for?
  • ΔT = −1.4 °C
  • Kcr = 1.86 °C·kg/osmol
  • MNaCl = 58.5 g/mol
  • V = 1 L
Hint

The freezing point gives the total osmolarity (every particle counts). The masses give the osmolarity of NaCl and urea only. Compare.

Solution

1) Osmolarities

  1. Law Cryoscopic depression p. 1
    ΔTsol = −Kcr · ω ⇒ ω = |ΔT| / Kcr
    ωtotal = 1.4 / 1.86 = 0.753 osmol/L

    K is given per kg (osmolality); for dilute urine 1 kg of water ≈ 1 L, so we read the result as osmol/L.

  2. Law Osmolarity p. 1
    ω = [1 + α(β − 1)] · C
    NaCl: C = 11.7 / 58.5 = 0.200 mol/L, β = 2, α = 1 → ω = 0.400 osmol/L
    Urea (non-electrolyte): C = 15 / 60 = 0.250 mol/L → ω = 0.250 osmol/L
ωtotal ≈ 753 mosmol/L · ωNaCl = 400 mosmol/L · ωurea = 250 mosmol/L
The sheet gives 393 mosmol/L for NaCl. With MNaCl = 23 + 35.5 = 58.5 g/mol, 11.7 g is exactly 0.2 mol → 400 mosmol/L; 393 corresponds to M = 59.5 g/mol, probably a slip in the key. Use the molar mass your teacher gives.

2) Conclusion

  1. ωexcess = 753 − 400 − 250 ≈ 103 mosmol/L (≈ 110 with the sheet's 393)

    NaCl and urea don't explain all the urine's particles: about 0.1 osmol/L comes from another solute.

  2. Albumin doesn't dissociate (ω = C), so it would need:

    m = 0.103 mol/L × 70 000 g/mol ≈ 7 200 g/L

    — impossible (more than 7 kg per litre). Being so heavy, albumin brings very few osmoles per gram: it can't be the cause.

  3. So look for a small, abundant molecule — typically glucose (glycosuria, as in diabetes).

Excess ≈ 0.1 osmol/L — not albumin (would need ≈ 7 kg/L); look for glucose
"Glucose in the urine of a diabetic" is the expected clinical answer but isn't taught in these lessons — it's general knowledge.
Traps
  • ΔT is negative: ω = |ΔT| / K.
  • Forgetting β = 2 for NaCl (ω = 0.2 instead of 0.4).
  • Big molecules (proteins) weigh a lot but count little in osmolarity — colligative properties count particles, not mass.

Exercise 5 — Molar mass by cryoscopy sheet

4.6 g of ethyl alcohol (M = 46 g/mol) in 200 mL of pure solvent gives a solution that begins to deposit ice at −0.93 °C. Next, 5.8 g of a non-ionizable compound of unknown molar mass is dissolved in 100 mL of the same solvent; ice starts to deposit at −0.59 °C. Calculate the molar mass of the unknown substance. Freezing point of the pure solvent: 0 °C.

  • malc = 4.6 g in 0.2 L, ΔT1 = −0.93 °C
  • mX = 5.8 g in 0.1 L, ΔT2 = −0.59 °C
  • both non-electrolytes
Hint

Kcr of this solvent isn't given: find it with the alcohol solution. Then use it to get ω of the unknown, hence its moles.

Solution
  1. Law Cryoscopic depression p. 1
    ΔTsol = −Kcr · ω (non-electrolyte: ω = C)
    Alcohol: C = (4.6 / 46) / 0.2 = 0.1 / 0.2 = 0.5 mol/L
    Kcr = |ΔT1| / ω = 0.93 / 0.5 = 1.86 °C·L/osmol

    That's water's constant: the solvent is water.

  2. Unknown: ω = 0.59 / 1.86 = 0.317 osmol/L = C
    n = C × V = 0.317 × 0.1 = 3.17 × 10⁻² mol
  3. M = m / n = 5.8 / 3.17 × 10⁻² = 183 g/mol
M ≈ 183 g/mol

Same as the sheet's answer. The solution volume is taken equal to the solvent volume (dilute solutions).

Traps
  • Concentration = moles ÷ volume in litres: 0.1 mol in 200 mL is 0.5 mol/L, not 0.1.
  • Different volumes (200 vs 100 mL) in the two solutions — don't reuse the first one.

Exercise 6 — Dissociation coefficient from iso-osmolarity sheet

A 2.24 M ammonia (NH4OH) solution is iso-osmotic to a 1.4 M sodium chloride solution. Determine the dissociation coefficient α of NH4OH. The membrane is considered semi-permeable.

  • CNH₄OH = 2.24 mol/L
  • CNaCl = 1.4 mol/L, α = 1
  • semi-permeable membrane
Hint

Iso-osmotic → equal osmolarities. NaCl is fully dissociated; NH4OH (→ NH4⁺ + OH⁻) is a weak electrolyte: keep α in the formula.

Solution
  1. Law Iso-osmotic, semi-permeable membrane p. 4
    ωNH₄OH = ωNaCl
  2. Law Osmolarity p. 1
    ω = [1 + α(β − 1)] · C
    NaCl: ω = 2 × 1.4 = 2.8 osmol/L
    NH4OH (β = 2): 2.24 × (1 + α) = 2.8
    1 + α = 2.8 / 2.24 = 1.25 → α = 0.25
α = 0.25 (25 % of NH4OH is dissociated)

Same as the sheet's answer. 0 ≤ α ≤ 1 and α < 1, as expected for a weak electrolyte.

Traps
  • Iso-osmotic means same osmolarity, not same molarity (2.24 ≠ 1.4).
  • With α < 1, use 1 + α(β − 1), not α·β.
