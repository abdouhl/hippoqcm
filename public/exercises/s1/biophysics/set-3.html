Exercise 1 — Magnesium from the electrolyte balance sheet

The equivalent concentration of anions in an intracellular fluid is 170 mEq/L. The only cations whose concentrations are high enough to contribute to the overall electrolyte balance are Na⁺ (23 g/mol), K⁺ (39 g/mol) and Mg²⁺ (24 g/mol). Given that CpNa⁺ = 0.46 g/L and CpK⁺ = 4.48 g/L, calculate CpMg²⁺.

  • Ce− = 170 mEq/L
  • CpNa⁺ = 0.46 g/L, M = 23 g/mol
  • CpK⁺ = 4.48 g/L, M = 39 g/mol
  • MMg = 24 g/mol
Hint

The fluid is electrically neutral: the cations carry as many equivalents as the anions. Convert each mass concentration into mEq/L (watch the valence of Mg²⁺), and Mg²⁺ supplies what is missing.

Solution
  1. Law Equivalent concentration p. 1
    C = Cp / M , Ce = |z| C
    [Na⁺] = 0.46 / 23 = 0.020 mol/L = 20 mmol/L ⇒ Ce(Na⁺) = 1 × 20 = 20 mEq/L
    [K⁺] = 4.48 / 39 = 0.1149 mol/L = 114.87 mmol/L ⇒ Ce(K⁺) = 114.87 mEq/L
  2. Law Electroneutrality p. 1
    Ce+ = Ce(Na⁺) + Ce(K⁺) + Ce(Mg²⁺) = Ce−
    Ce(Mg²⁺) = 170 − 20 − 114.87 = 35.13 mEq/L

  3. Mg²⁺ has z = 2: one mole carries 2 Eq.

    [Mg²⁺] = 35.13 / 2 = 17.56 mmol/L
    CpMg²⁺ = 17.56 × 10⁻³ × 24 = 0.421 g/L
CpMg²⁺ ≈ 0.42 g/L (Ce = 35.1 mEq/L, 17.6 mmol/L)
Traps
  • Forgetting z = 2 for Mg²⁺: 35.13 × 24 / 1000 = 0.84 g/L is twice the answer.
  • Adding the masses in g/L: electroneutrality is written in equivalents, never in g/L or in mol/L.

Exercise 2 — Ions and an undissociated macromolecule sheet

Two compartments (1) and (2) have the same volume, 1 L. Compartment (1) contains 100 mmol of NaCl and 2 mmol of an undissociated macromolecule; compartment (2) contains 99 mmol of KCl and 4 mmol of the same macromolecule.

  1. The two compartments are separated by a perfectly semi-permeable membrane. What is observed?
  2. The two compartments are separated by a membrane permeable to water and to inorganic ions only. Consider in turn the ionic fluxes and the flow of water.
    1. How will the inorganic ions be distributed after an infinite time?
    2. If this distribution is reached without any net fluid flow, can the system be in equilibrium? Why or why not?
    3. What must be done so that the liquid levels in the two compartments stay unchanged? At the temperature of the experiment, one mole of solute per litre exerts an osmotic pressure of 24 atm.
  • V1 = V2 = 1 L
  • (1): NaCl 100 mmol/L + macromolecule 2 mmol/L
  • (2): KCl 99 mmol/L + macromolecule 4 mmol/L
  • macromolecule undissociated (no charge)
  • RT = 24 atm per osmol/L
Hint

1) Only water crosses: compare the total osmolarities. 2) The macromolecule is not charged, so there is no Donnan effect: each ion simply diffuses until its concentration is the same on both sides. Then recompute the osmolarities — what is still unequal? The pressure that stops water is the osmotic pressure difference, applied on the side water goes to.

Solution

1) Perfectly semi-permeable membrane

  1. Law Osmolarity p. 2
    ω = Σ [1 + αi(βi − 1)] Ci

    NaCl and KCl are strong electrolytes (α = 1, β = 2); the macromolecule is undissociated (ω = C).

    ω1 = 2 × 100 + 2 = 202 mosmol/L
    ω2 = 2 × 99 + 4 = 202 mosmol/L
  2. Law Osmotic pressure p. 3
    Δπ = (ω2 − ω1) RT
    Δπ = (202 − 202) × RT = 0
The solutions are isotonic: no net water flow, nothing changes (levels stay equal)

2) a) Distribution of the inorganic ions

The macromolecule is undissociated, so it carries no charge: there is no Donnan effect. Each ion diffuses down its own gradient (Na⁺ and Cl⁻ from (1) to (2), K⁺ from (2) to (1)) until its concentration is the same on both sides (volumes stay 1 L).

  1. Law Diffusion to equilibrium Solutions 5 · p. 2
    C∞ = (C1i + C2i) / 2
    [Na⁺]1 = [Na⁺]2 = (100 + 0) / 2 = 50 mmol/L
    [K⁺]1 = [K⁺]2 = (0 + 99) / 2 = 49.5 mmol/L
    [Cl⁻]1 = [Cl⁻]2 = (100 + 99) / 2 = 99.5 mmol/L

    Check electroneutrality on each side: 50 + 49.5 = 99.5 mEq/L of cations = 99.5 mEq/L of Cl⁻ ✓

Each side: Na⁺ 50 mmol/L · K⁺ 49.5 mmol/L · Cl⁻ 99.5 mmol/L

2) b) Is it an equilibrium?

  1. ω1 = 50 + 49.5 + 99.5 + 2 = 201 mosmol/L
    ω2 = 50 + 49.5 + 99.5 + 4 = 203 mosmol/L
  2. Law Oncotic pressure Solutions 8 · p. 1
    Δπ = Δωi RT + Δωp RT

    The diffusible ions are now equal (Δωi = 0); only the macromolecule, which cannot cross, still differs:

    Δω = ω2 − ω1 = 4 − 2 = 2 mosmol/L = 0.002 osmol/L
    Δπ = 0.002 × 24 = 0.048 atm (≈ 4.86 × 10³ Pa ≈ 36.5 mmHg)
No: ω2 > ω1, so water still flows (1) → (2) under Δπ = 0.048 atm

The ions are in diffusion equilibrium, but not the water: the system is not in equilibrium. At the start (question 1) the excess of salt in (1) compensated the excess of macromolecule in (2); once the ions have equalised, only the macromolecule's oncotic pressure remains.

2) c) Keeping the levels unchanged

As in the lesson's Exercise 13 (Solutions 8 · p. 2), apply to the compartment that receives the water a pressure equal to Δπ, in the opposite direction.

P = Δπ = 0.048 atm, applied on compartment (2)
Exert an overpressure of 0.048 atm (≈ 36.5 mmHg) on compartment (2)
Assumed: no Donnan effect because the macromolecule is "undissociated" (neutral). Another way to keep the levels is to add 2 mmol/L of an impermeant solute to compartment (1) so that ω1 = ω2; the lesson's method (and the 24 atm the sheet gives) points to the counter-pressure. 0.048 atm → mmHg and Pa uses 1 atm = 760 mmHg = 101 325 Pa.
Traps
  • Concluding from question 1 that nothing ever happens: once ions can cross, the balance between salt and macromolecule breaks.
  • Counting the macromolecule as an electrolyte: undissociated ⇒ ω = C, and no Donnan effect.
  • Using 24 atm × 4 mmol/L: only the difference (2 mmol/L) creates the pressure.
  • Applying the pressure on (1): it goes on the side the water flows to, i.e. (2).

Exercise 3 — Donnan equilibrium with a charged macromolecule sheet

A container is divided into two compartments (1) and (2) by a dialysis membrane. A different NaCl solution is poured into each compartment, and one of them also contains a non-diffusible but electrolytic macromolecule. At equilibrium:

[Na⁺]1 · [Cl⁻]1 = 0.16 (osmol/L)² ; [Cl⁻]2 = 0.457 osmol/L.

  1. Calculate [Na⁺]2.
  2. On which side is the charged macromolecule? Give the sign of its charge and calculate its osmolarity, given that the absolute value of its valence is 18.
  3. Calculate the osmotic pressure exerted by one compartment on the other, at 17 °C.
  4. Calculate the Donnan potential and the transmembrane field, given that the membrane is 10 µm thick. Draw this field.
  • [Na⁺]1[Cl⁻]1 = 0.16 (osmol/L)²
  • [Cl⁻]2 = 0.457 osmol/L
  • |z| = 18
  • T = 17 °C = 290 K
  • l = 10 µm = 10⁻⁵ m
  • R = 8.31 J/(mol·K), F = 96 500 C
Hint

The Donnan equation for Na⁺ and Cl⁻ says the product [Na⁺][Cl⁻] is the same on both sides. In the compartment without the macromolecule, [Na⁺] = [Cl⁻]. In the other one, electroneutrality tells you what charge the macro-ion must carry. Then: Δω → Δπ, Nernst → potential, E = −ΔV/l.

Solution

1) [Na⁺]2

  1. Law Donnan equilibrium Solutions 7 · p. 4
    [Cl⁻]2[Cl⁻]1 = [Na⁺]1[Na⁺]2 ⇔ [Na⁺]1[Cl⁻]1 = [Na⁺]2[Cl⁻]2
    [Na⁺]2 = 0.16 / 0.457 = 0.350 osmol/L
[Na⁺]2 ≈ 0.350 osmol/L

2) Side, sign and osmolarity of the macromolecule

  1. In (2), [Cl⁻]2 = 0.457 > [Na⁺]2 = 0.350: the anions outnumber Na⁺, so another cation must balance them — the macromolecule is in (2) and it is positive (Pr18+). Compartment (1) has only NaCl:

    [Na⁺]1 = [Cl⁻]1 = √0.16 = 0.400 osmol/L
  2. Law Electroneutrality Solutions 7 · p. 1
    [Na⁺]2 + z C′ = [Cl⁻]2
    z C′ = 0.457 − 0.350 = 0.1069 Eq/L
    C′ = 0.1069 / 18 = 5.94 × 10⁻³ mol/L

    The macro-ion is one particle: its osmolarity is ωp = C′ (its counter-ions, the Cl⁻, are already counted in [Cl⁻]2).

In (2), positive (z = +18), ωp ≈ 5.94 × 10⁻³ osmol/L (5.94 mosmol/L)

3) Osmotic pressure

  1. Law Osmolarity Solutions 3 · p. 2
    ω = Σ ωi
    ω1 = 0.400 + 0.400 = 0.800 osmol/L
    ω2 = 0.350 + 0.457 + 0.00594 = 0.8130 osmol/L
    Δω = 0.8130 − 0.800 = 0.01305 osmol/L = 13.05 osmol/m³
  2. Law Oncotic pressure Solutions 8 · p. 1
    Δπ = (ω2 − ω1) RT = ΔωiRT + ωpRT
    Δπ = 13.05 × 8.31 × 290 = 3.14 × 10⁴ Pa (≈ 0.31 atm)

    Split: Δωi = 0.807 − 0.800 = 7.1 × 10⁻³ osmol/L from the unequal ions, ωp = 5.94 × 10⁻³ osmol/L from the macromolecule.

Δπ ≈ 3.14 × 10⁴ Pa, exerted by (2) on (1): water is drawn (1) → (2)

4) Donnan potential and transmembrane field

  1. Law Nernst equation Solutions 7 · p. 3
    V2 − V1 = − RTzF ln C2C1
    RT/F = 8.31 × 290 / 96 500 = 0.02497 V
    Na⁺ (z = +1): V2 − V1 = −0.02497 × ln(0.350 / 0.400) = −0.02497 × (−0.1332) = +3.33 × 10⁻³ V
    Cl⁻ (z = −1): V2 − V1 = +0.02497 × ln(0.457 / 0.400) = 0.02497 × 0.1332 = +3.33 × 10⁻³ V ✓
  2. Law Transmembrane field Solutions 7 · p. 3
    E = − (V2 − V1) / l
    E = − 3.33 × 10⁻³ / 10⁻⁵ = −333 V/m

    V2 > V1: the face of the membrane in contact with the positive macro-ions (side 2) is (+), side (1) is (−). The field goes from (+) to (−), i.e. from (2) to (1) — the minus sign means it points against the (1) → (2) axis.

V2 − V1 ≈ +3.33 mV · E ≈ −333 V/m (333 V/m directed from (2) to (1))
17 °C is taken as 290 K (273 + 17) with R = 8.31, as in Set 2; with 290.15 K and R = 8.314 the results change only in the 3rd digit. The sheet writes "17°" without a unit: Celsius is assumed. The sheet gives no answers to compare with.
Traps
  • Placing the macro-ion in the side with more Na⁺: with a positive macro-ion it's the Cl⁻ that are in excess on its side.
  • Counting the macromolecule as 18 × C′ osmoles: 18 is its charge; as one particle it counts once (ωp = C′).
  • Forgetting z = −1 for Cl⁻ in Nernst: both ions must give the same V2 − V1 — a good check.
  • T in °C, ω in osmol/L instead of osmol/m³, or l in µm instead of m.
