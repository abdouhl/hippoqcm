If you remember only this
  • Passive transport: no ATP, down the (electro)chemical gradient — simple diffusion, facilitated diffusion (channels, carriers), osmosis.
  • Simple diffusion needs no protein: small, non-polar, lipid-soluble molecules (O₂, CO₂, steroids, vitamins A D E K). Fick: rate ↑ with surface, gradient, permeability, temperature; ↓ with membrane thickness (fibrosis, oedema).
  • Facilitated diffusion uses specific proteins: channels (pores, very fast, gated) or carriers (bind and change shape, slower, saturable — GLUT).
  • Primary active: uses ATP directly (pumps). Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase: 3 Na⁺ out, 2 K⁺ in per ATP, electrogenic; inhibited by digoxin.
  • Secondary active: uses the Na⁺ gradient made by the pump, not ATP directly; symport (same direction: SGLT1 2 Na⁺ + 1 glucose) or antiport (opposite: NHE, NCX).
  • Osmosis: water moves towards the higher solute concentration. Hypertonic → cell shrinks (crenation); hypotonic → swells, may burst.
  • Vesicular transport needs ATP: endocytosis (phagocytosis, pinocytosis, receptor-mediated) and exocytosis (SNARE, Ca²⁺).

1. Definition and functions of the plasma membrane p. 2

Plasma (cell) membrane A thin biological membrane, about 7–10 nm thick, surrounding every living cell; it separates the intracellular environment (cytoplasm) from the extracellular one. Not a passive wall: a dynamic, highly organized, selective barrier that controls exchanges.

2. Molecular architecture of the membrane p. 3–5

Three components: lipids (phospholipids, cholesterol), proteins (integral, peripheral), carbohydrates (glycocalyx, outer surface only). Lipid rafts: small domains rich in cholesterol and sphingolipids that gather receptors and signalling proteins.

2.1 Phospholipids and the bilayer

Each phospholipid = a hydrophilic head (phosphate + alcohol) and two hydrophobic tails (fatty acids). In water they form a bilayer: heads face the water on both sides, tails meet in the middle. The hydrophobic core is the barrier to ions and polar molecules.

2.2 Cholesterol

Inserted between phospholipids: small polar OH near the heads, rigid steroid ring and tail among the fatty-acid tails.

  • Buffers fluidity: ↑ temperature → limits excessive fluidity; ↓ temperature → prevents solidifying.
  • Decreases permeability to ions and small polar molecules.
  • Modulates proteins and forms lipid rafts.
Clinical application Atherosclerosis: LDL-derived cholesterol accumulates in arterial walls → plaque. Statins inhibit HMG-CoA reductase → ↓ hepatic cholesterol synthesis → ↑ hepatic LDL receptors → ↓ circulating LDL.

2.3 Membrane proteins

Integral proteins are embedded in the bilayer (many are transmembrane); peripheral proteins sit on one surface and do not cross it.

2.4–2.5 Glycocalyx and the fluid mosaic model

  • Glycocalyx: sugar chains on proteins (glycoproteins) or lipids (glycolipids), only on the outer surface: recognition (cellular ID card — blood groups), protection, adhesion.
  • Fluid mosaic model: a mosaic of proteins floating in a fluid lipid bilayer, components moving constantly → flexibility, self-repair, working proteins, membrane fusion (division, vesicles), right fluidity for transport and signalling.
  • The membrane is asymmetric: outer face = glycocalyx + specific lipids/proteins; inner face = different lipids/proteins, linked to the cytoskeleton.

4. Passive transport p. 6–8 2022-23 · 2024-25 · 2025-26

No ATP: substances move down their concentration gradient (for ions, their electrochemical gradient), using the kinetic energy of molecules. Net movement stops at equilibrium.

4.1 Simple diffusion

Directly through the phospholipid bilayer, without any transport protein: approaches → dissolves in the bilayer ("oil in oil") → crosses the core → emerges → equilibrium (no further net movement).

MoleculesExamples
Small, non-polar, lipid-solubleO₂, CO₂, N₂
Steroid hormonesCortisol, oestrogens, testosterone
Fat-soluble vitaminsA, D, E, K
Small uncharged moleculesEthanol, urea (slowly); water (slowly — mostly via aquaporins)
Lipid-soluble drugsGeneral anaesthetics; non-ionized weak acids/bases (aspirin)
Diffusion rate ∝ surface area × concentration difference × permeabilitymembrane thickness Fick's law · permeability ↑ with lipid solubility, ↓ with molecular size · temperature ↑ molecular movement

4.2 Facilitated diffusion 2023-24 · 2024-25 · 2025-26

For substances too large (glucose, amino acids) or charged (Na⁺, K⁺, Ca²⁺, Cl⁻) to cross the core: down the gradient, through specific transport proteins, no ATP. Two types: channels and carriers.

a) Channels form water-filled pores: highly selective (each type passes specific ions), often gated, very fast (up to millions of ions/s).

GateOpens in response toExample
Voltage-gatedChange in membrane potentialNa⁺ channels of the nerve impulse
Ligand-gatedBinding of a specific moleculeNicotinic ACh receptor at the neuromuscular junction
Mechanically gatedStretch or pressureTouch receptors, hair cells of the inner ear

b) Carriers do not form a pore: they bind the molecule and change shape to release it on the other side (bind → change shape → release → return, repeat). Highly specific, slower, regulated, saturable. Main example: the GLUT family.

4.3 Osmosis 2024-25

Osmosis Passive movement of water across a selectively permeable membrane, from the side with low solute concentration (more water) to the side with high solute concentration (less water). Slowly through the bilayer, rapidly through aquaporins (water channels that block ions and solutes).
Red blood cell in…SolutionWaterResult
Isotonic (e.g. 0.9 % NaCl)—in = outNormal shape
HypertonicMore solute outsideLeaves the cellShrinks (crenation)
HypotonicLess solute outsideEnters the cellSwells, may burst (haemolysis)

In the body: water balance between red cells and plasma, kidney water reabsorption (aquaporin-2, regulated by ADH), intestinal absorption — hence IV fluids must have the right tonicity.

5. Active transport p. 9–10 2022-23 · 2023-24 · 2024-25 · 2025-26

Moves substances against their concentration or electrochemical gradient (low → high); needs energy and specific proteins. Roles: ion balance (Na⁺, K⁺, Ca²⁺), pH, membrane potential; nutrient absorption (glucose, amino acids, Ca²⁺, Fe²⁺); export of toxins and drugs (ABC transporters); gastric acid secretion.

5.1 Primary active transport

The transporter uses direct hydrolysis of ATP (it is an ATPase, a "pump").

  1. 3 Na⁺ bind to the pump on the inside.
  2. ATP → ADP: the pump is phosphorylated and changes shape.
  3. 3 Na⁺ are released outside.
  4. 2 K⁺ bind on the outside.
  5. Dephosphorylation: the pump returns to its original shape.
  6. 2 K⁺ are released inside; the pump can bind Na⁺ again. One cycle = 1 ATP.

Each cycle moves one net positive charge out: the pump is electrogenic. Roles: keeps the Na⁺/K⁺ gradients and the resting potential, controls cell volume, and creates the Na⁺ gradient used by secondary active transport.

Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase3 Na⁺ out, 2 K⁺ inAll cells (basolateral in epithelia)Gradients, volume, RMP. Inhibited by digoxin

5.2 Secondary active transport (cotransport)

Uses the energy stored in an ion gradient, usually the Na⁺ gradient created by the Na⁺/K⁺ pump: the ion moving down its gradient drives another substance against its gradient (coupling). The transporter does not use ATP itself, but stops if the pump stops.

Symport (cotransport)Antiport (exchange)
DirectionIon and substance in the same directionOpposite directions
ExamplesSGLT1, SGLT2 (Na⁺ + glucose); Na⁺-amino acids; Na⁺-K⁺-2Cl⁻ (NKCC); Na⁺-bile salts; Na⁺-neurotransmitter reuptake Na⁺/H⁺ exchanger (NHE: pH); Na⁺/Ca²⁺ exchanger (NCX: removes Ca²⁺)
Clinical links
  • Oral rehydration solution contains glucose + Na⁺: SGLT1 absorbs both and water follows by osmosis — why it works in cholera and infant diarrhoea.
  • SGLT2 inhibitors (gliflozins) block glucose reabsorption in the proximal tubule: type 2 diabetes and heart failure.

6. Vesicular (bulk) transport p. 11–12

Moves large molecules, particles or volumes of fluid inside membrane vesicles: molecules too big for transporters (proteins, polysaccharides), particles (bacteria, debris), bulk secretion (hormones, enzymes, neurotransmitters). Requires ATP, highly regulated, works in both directions.

6.1 Endocytosis

Invaginationvesicle formation (pinches off)trafficking into the cell
  • Phagocytosis ("cell eating"): pseudopods surround the particle → phagosome → fusion with a lysosome = phagolysosome → degradation (enzymes, reactive oxygen species). Professional phagocytes: neutrophils, macrophages, dendritic cells.
  • Receptor-mediated ("selective uptake"): ligand binds its receptor → clathrin-coated pit → dynamin pinches it off → uncoating → early endosome: ligand degraded or used, receptor recycled to the surface.

Exam traps — the wrong options past exams used

False statements to recognise
  • Passive transport "requires ATP" / "always occurs against the gradient" — false. 2025-26
  • Assisted (mediated) transport "consists of simple diffusion" — false: simple diffusion uses no protein. 2022-23
  • Facilitated diffusion "requires metabolic energy" — false (it is specific, saturable, down the gradient). 2025-26
  • A substance can go against its gradient by "facilitated diffusion", "simple diffusion" or "ion channels" — false: only active transport. 2023-24
  • "Na⁺–glucose" transport is primary active — false: it is secondary (Ca²⁺-, H⁺/K⁺- and Na⁺/K⁺-ATPases are primary). 2023-24
  • Primary active transport "occurs without membrane proteins" / "is always electrically neutral" — false: the Na⁺/K⁺ pump is electrogenic. 2025-26
  • The Na⁺/K⁺ pump "functions without ATP" — false. 2025-26
  • Secondary active transport "directly uses ATP" / "needs no membrane proteins"; SGLT1 is "an antiport" — false. 2024-25 · 2025-26
  • GLUT2 in hyperglycaemia "actively transports glucose with ATP" / "prevents glucose entry" — false: it lets glucose enter, down its gradient. 2024-25
  • A thickened alveolar wall (pulmonary fibrosis) "increases O₂ diffusion" — false: thickness slows diffusion. 2024-25
  • Red cells in a hypertonic solution "swell and burst" — false: they shrink; swelling is hypotonic. 2024-25
  • Voltage-gated channels "are always open" — false: activated by voltage changes, selective for one ion type. 2023-24

Self-check — answer, then tap to reveal

What are the four functions of the plasma membrane?
Protection, selective transport, signalling and communication, cell recognition.
Which phospholipid signals apoptosis, and how?
Phosphatidylserine (inner leaflet): its exposure on the outer surface is the "eat-me" signal.
Which phospholipid movement is very rare, and why?
Flip-flop: the polar head would have to cross the hydrophobic core; needs flippases/scramblases.
Three roles of cholesterol in the membrane?
Buffers fluidity, decreases permeability to ions/small polar molecules, modulates proteins and forms lipid rafts.
Give 3 kinds of molecule that cross by simple diffusion.
Gases (O₂, CO₂), steroid hormones, fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K) — also ethanol, urea slowly, lipid-soluble drugs.
What slows simple diffusion, according to Fick?
A thicker membrane (fibrosis, oedema), larger molecules, smaller gradient, surface or temperature.
Channel vs carrier: which saturates?
The carrier (Vmax when all are busy); channels are very fast pores, rarely limiting.
A red cell in a hypertonic solution?
Water leaves → it shrinks (crenation).
Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase: stoichiometry and why it is electrogenic?
3 Na⁺ out, 2 K⁺ in per ATP → one net positive charge out per cycle.
Which drugs inhibit the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase, the H⁺/K⁺-ATPase and SGLT2?
Digoxin; proton pump inhibitors (omeprazole); gliflozins.
What powers secondary active transport?
The Na⁺ gradient made by the Na⁺/K⁺ pump — not ATP directly; it stops if the pump stops.
Symport or antiport: SGLT1, NKCC, NHE, NCX?
SGLT1 and NKCC: symport. NHE and NCX: antiport.
Why does oral rehydration solution contain glucose and Na⁺?
SGLT1 absorbs both together, and water follows by osmosis.
What goes wrong in familial hypercholesterolaemia?
The LDL receptor is mutated: no receptor-mediated endocytosis of LDL → cholesterol stays in the blood → early atherosclerosis.
What triggers regulated exocytosis, and which proteins mediate fusion?
A rise in Ca²⁺; SNARE proteins.
