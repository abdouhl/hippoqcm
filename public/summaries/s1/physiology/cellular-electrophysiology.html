If you remember only this
  • RMP (inside negative, neuron ≈ −70 mV; not the same in all cells) = ion gradients kept by the Na⁺/K⁺ pump + selective permeability (PK ≫ PNa) + impermeant intracellular anions (Gibbs–Donnan). It lies close to EK (−90 mV) because K⁺ leak dominates. NaV channels are closed at rest.
  • Nernst = equilibrium potential of one ion: E ≈ (61.5/z)·log([out]/[in]) mV at 37 °C. EK ≈ −90, ENa ≈ +61, ECl ≈ −66, ECa ≈ +125 mV. GHK = membrane potential for several ions.
  • Driving force = Vm − Eion; current I = g(Vm − Eion).
  • AP: threshold ≈ −55 mV → NaV open, Na⁺ in (to ≈ +30 mV) → NaV inactivate, KV open, K⁺ out → afterhyperpolarization (KV close slowly) → rest.
  • All-or-none: above threshold the AP is the same size; stimulus strength is coded by firing frequency. Absolute refractory period (NaV inactivated) → no AP possible; relative → stronger stimulus needed.
  • TTX and lidocaine block NaV; TEA blocks K⁺ channels.
  • Myelinated = saltatory (node to node), faster (2–120 m/s), cheaper; velocity ↑ with myelin, diameter, temperature.
  • Graded potentials: local, amplitude ∝ stimulus, decrease with distance, summable, no threshold, no refractory period, depolarizing or hyperpolarizing.

1. Introduction p. 2–3

Cellular electrophysiology studies the electrical properties of cells and tissues: how signals are generated, transmitted and recorded (neuronal communication, sensory perception, muscle contraction, heart rhythm, neurosecretion).

SystemElectrical functionDisorderRecording
BrainNeuronal communicationEpilepsyEEG
Peripheral nerve and muscleConduction and contractionNeuropathies, myopathiesENMG: EMG and nerve conduction studies
HeartRhythm and coordinated contractionArrhythmiasECG

A pacemaker delivers impulses to support the heart rhythm; cardiac ablation destroys tissue that generates or conducts abnormal rhythms.

Excitable cell Responds to a sufficient stimulus by changing its membrane potential and can generate action potentials; excitability depends on ion gradients and ion channels. Four types: neuron (APs carry information), skeletal muscle fibre (APs trigger contraction), smooth muscle cell (many generate APs), cardiac muscle cell (APs coordinate the heartbeat).
Sufficient stimulusdepolarizationaction potentialresponse: signal, contraction, secretion

3. Electrical properties of excitable membranes p. 4

4. Resting membrane potential p. 5–8 2022-23 · 2023-24 · 2025-26

RMP The voltage inside a cell relative to the extracellular fluid at rest, in mV (−70 mV = inside 70 mV negative). All cells have one, even those that never fire.
CategoryCellApproximate RMP
ExcitableNeuron≈ −70 mV
Skeletal muscle fibre≈ −85 to −90 mV
Ventricular muscle cell≈ −85 to −90 mV
Non-excitableRed blood cell≈ −10 mV
Epithelial cell, fibroblast, adipocyte≈ −20 to −50 mV (very variable)

A negative RMP alone does not make a cell excitable — the channel types matter. Cardiac pacemaker cells have no stable resting potential.

Demonstration (squid giant axon): both electrodes outside → 0 mV; the recording microelectrode penetrates the axon → stable negative voltage (≈ −60 to −70 mV) = the RMP.

Origin of the resting potential

Unequal ion concentrations + selective permeability: a concentration difference alone creates no voltage unless the membrane lets one ion cross more easily. K⁺ is concentrated inside; Na⁺, Cl⁻ and free Ca²⁺ outside; impermeant anions (proteins⁻) are high inside.

  • Chemical force: moves an ion from high to low concentration.
  • Electrical force: depends on voltage and charge; a negative interior attracts cations, repels anions.
  • Together = the electrochemical gradient. Movement also needs an open channel for that ion.
  1. Membrane permeable only to K⁺: K⁺ leaves down its gradient (Vm = 0, net efflux).
  2. Charge separates: the inside becomes negative; the electrical force pulls K⁺ back.
  3. Equilibrium: chemical = electrical force, no net K⁺ movement, Vm = EK ≈ −90 mV. The gradient still exists and ions still cross both ways at equal rates; very few ions need to move.
Eion = RTzF · ln [ion]out[ion]in    at 37 °C: Eion ≈ 61.5z · log₁₀ [ion]out[ion]in mV Nernst equation · z = signed charge: for Cl⁻ z = −1 reverses the sign; for Ca²⁺ z = +2 halves the factor (≈ 30.8 mV)

Worked example: EK = 61.5 × log₁₀(4.8/140) = 61.5 × (−1.46) ≈ −90 mV.

Three mechanisms behind the RMP

MechanismWhat it does
1. Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase3 Na⁺ out, 2 K⁺ in per ATP: creates and maintains the gradients; electrogenic (adds a few mV of negativity)
2. Selective permeabilityAt rest PK ≫ PNa (many K⁺ leak channels, few Na⁺ leaks; NaV closed but available)
3. Impermeant intracellular anionsProteins, nucleic acids, organic phosphates can't leave: shape the ion distribution (Gibbs–Donnan)

Because K⁺ leak dominates, the RMP lies close to EK; the small Na⁺ leak pulls it towards ENa → ≈ −70 mV. Only a tiny charge excess next to the membrane is needed; bulk fluids stay almost neutral.

Vm = 61.5 · log₁₀ PK[K⁺]out + PNa[Na⁺]out + PCl[Cl⁻]inPK[K⁺]in + PNa[Na⁺]in + PCl[Cl⁻]out Goldman–Hodgkin–Katz · with PK : PNa = 1 : 0.05 (Cl⁻ ignored) → Vm ≈ −66 mV · the most permeant ion pulls Vm towards its own Eion
Clinical link — plasma potassium Hyperkalaemia → EK less negative → RMP depolarizes → prolonged depolarization inactivates NaV → ↓ excitability: arrhythmias, muscle weakness. Hypokalaemia → EK more negative → hyperpolarization, also impairing muscle and heart.

5. The action potential p. 9–11 2022-23 · 2023-24 · 2024-25 · 2025-26

Action potential A rapid, transient, regenerative reversal of the membrane potential, triggered when depolarization reaches threshold; all-or-none. Squid-axon demonstration: a subthreshold stimulus gives only a small local response; a suprathreshold one gives a full AP of constant amplitude and duration.
PhaseVoltageNaVKVDominant current
Rest≈ −70 mVClosed, availableClosed (leak K⁺ open)Small K⁺ leak out
Depolarization−55 → ≈ +30 mVOpen (fast activation)Mostly still closedNa⁺ in
Repolarization+30 → −70 mVInactivatedOpen (delayed)K⁺ out
AfterhyperpolarizationBelow −70 mVRecovering (closed)Still open, closing slowlyK⁺ out
Return to rest≈ −70 mVClosed, availableClosedLeak; pump maintains gradients

The AP reflects a change in permeability, first to Na⁺, then to K⁺; the two currents overlap in time.

depolarization reaches thresholdmore NaV openNa⁺ entersfurther depolarization (positive feedback)

The loop is stopped by NaV inactivation and the delayed opening of KV.

Voltage clamp and patch clamp

The voltage clamp holds the voltage at a chosen value; the injected current equals and opposes the ionic current, which can thus be measured. Convention: inward (Na⁺) current downwards (negative), outward (K⁺) upwards (positive).

Step to 0 mVEarly currentLate currentConclusion
ControlTransient inwardSustained outwardTwo separate currents
+ TTX (blocks NaV)AbsentOutward remainsLate current = K⁺ (IK)
+ TEA (blocks KV)Inward remainsAbsentEarly current = Na⁺ (INa)

gNa rises very fast then falls by inactivation even if depolarization persists (rising phase); gK (delayed rectifier) rises more slowly and stays high longer (falling phase, afterhyperpolarization). Patch clamp: a glass micropipette seals onto a small patch; records a single channel (square current steps) or the whole cell; many single-channel openings sum to the macroscopic currents.

6. Conduction of the action potential p. 12–13 2022-23

Recording at increasing distances: the AP appears later (latency ↑) but with the same amplitude — it is regenerated, not fading.

Conduction velocity = distancedifference in latency e.g. sites 10 mm and 30 mm, latencies 0.5 and 1.5 ms → v = 20 mm / 1.0 ms = 20 m/s

Mechanism: positive charge entering the active region spreads inside the axon (local current) and depolarizes the next segment → threshold → its NaV open → AP regenerated. The segment behind is refractory, so the impulse moves forward only (a stimulus in the middle of an axon sends APs both ways).

Unmyelinated axonMyelinated axon
ModeContinuous: every patch regenerates the APSaltatory: regenerated only at the nodes of Ranvier ("jumps")
Velocity≈ 0.5–2 m/s≈ 2–120 m/s
Energy costHigher (ion exchange along the whole membrane)Lower (only at the nodes)
ExampleC fibres (slow pain, autonomic)Motor fibres, large sensory fibres

Velocity increases with myelination, larger diameter (lower internal resistance) and higher temperature (within physiological limits).

7. Graded potentials p. 13 2022-23 · 2023-24 · 2024-25

Graded potential A local change in membrane potential whose amplitude depends on the strength and duration of the stimulus; it can depolarize or hyperpolarize, spreads passively and decreases with distance, is not all-or-none. Occurs in dendrites, soma, sensory receptors, motor end plate.
  • Temporal summation: successive inputs at the same synapse, close enough in time to overlap.
  • Spatial summation: inputs at different synapses at the same time.
  • At the initial segment EPSPs add, IPSPs subtract; if the sum reaches threshold → AP.

Exam traps — the wrong options past exams used

False statements to recognise
  • The RMP is mainly determined by the "Na⁺", "Cl⁻" or "Ca²⁺" gradient — false: the K⁺ gradient. 2022-23
  • "Voltage-gated Na⁺ channels" are part of the ionic origin of the RMP — false (pump, Gibbs–Donnan and selective permeability are). 2023-24
  • The RMP "is identical in all cell types" — false. 2025-26
  • The Nernst equation calculates "the membrane potential" or "membrane permeability" — false: the equilibrium potential of one ion (GHK gives Vm). 2025-26
  • All-or-none means the AP "is triggered for any intensity", "changes amplitude with the stimulus" or "can be partially triggered" — false. 2023-24
  • The AP "depends on stimulus duration" — false: a longer stimulus changes frequency, not the AP. 2025-26
  • During the falling phase NaV channels "stay open" or "reopen" — false: they inactivate; once closed (recovered) they can open after a new stimulus. 2024-25
  • Voltage-gated channels "are always open" / "are involved in protein synthesis" — false. 2023-24
  • "TEA", "curare" or "GABA" block Na⁺ channels — false: TTX does (TEA blocks K⁺ channels). 2022-23
  • Myelinated conduction "is slower" / "continuous" — false: saltatory and faster. 2022-23
  • Graded potentials "occur in any region", "have an amplitude independent of the stimulus", "have a threshold" — false. 2023-24
  • Postsynaptic potentials are "all-or-none" or "propagated, always hyperpolarizing" — false: gradable and summable. 2022-23 · 2023-24 · 2024-25

Self-check — answer, then tap to reveal

Name the four excitable cell types.
Neuron, skeletal muscle fibre, smooth muscle cell, cardiac muscle cell.
Who makes myelin in the PNS and in the CNS?
Schwann cells (PNS), oligodendrocytes (CNS).
RMP of a neuron, a skeletal muscle fibre and a red blood cell?
≈ −70 mV; ≈ −85 to −90 mV; ≈ −10 mV.
Three mechanisms behind the RMP?
Na⁺/K⁺ pump (gradients, electrogenic), selective permeability (PK ≫ PNa), impermeant intracellular anions (Gibbs–Donnan).
Why is the RMP close to EK but not equal to it?
K⁺ leak dominates, but a small Na⁺ leak pulls Vm towards ENa (≈ −70 vs −90 mV).
Nernst at 37 °C; EK with [K⁺] 4.8 out and 140 in?
E ≈ (61.5/z) log([out]/[in]); EK ≈ 61.5 × (−1.46) ≈ −90 mV.
Driving force on Na⁺ at −70 mV, and its direction?
−70 − (+61) = −131 mV → strong entry.
What does hyperkalaemia do to the membrane?
EK less negative → depolarization → NaV inactivation → ↓ excitability (arrhythmias, weakness).
Channel states during the rising and falling phases?
Rising: NaV open, Na⁺ in. Falling: NaV inactivated, KV open, K⁺ out; KV close slowly → afterhyperpolarization.
In voltage clamp, what does TTX remove? TEA?
TTX: the early inward Na⁺ current. TEA: the late outward K⁺ current.
How is stimulus strength coded if the AP is all-or-none?
By firing frequency.
Absolute vs relative refractory period?
Absolute: no AP possible (NaV open/inactivated). Relative: stronger stimulus needed (some NaV inactivated, gK high).
Sites 20 mm and 50 mm, latencies 1.0 and 2.5 ms: velocity?
30 mm / 1.5 ms = 20 m/s.
Saltatory vs continuous conduction?
Saltatory (myelinated): AP regenerated at nodes, 2–120 m/s, cheaper. Continuous (unmyelinated, C fibres): 0.5–2 m/s.
Temporal vs spatial summation?
Temporal: successive inputs at the same synapse. Spatial: different synapses at the same time.
