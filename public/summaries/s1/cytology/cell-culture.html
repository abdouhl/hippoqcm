If you remember only this
  • Circulating cells (blood, lymph): a simple blood sample. Adherent cells (solid tissue): biopsy → mechanical → enzymatic dissociation → debris removal.
  • Primary culture: cells straight from the tissue, closest to the natural state, but all cell types mixed, few target cells, short lifespan (senescence → apoptosis).
  • Cell line: homogeneous, derived from a primary culture, resistant to apoptosis, divides indefinitely (immortalization, usually by viral genes).
  • Normal cell line = primary culture + flow cytometry sorting + immortalization. Tumor cell line = primary culture + sorting only (already apoptosis-resistant).
  • Flow cytometry: cells in suspension pass one by one through a LASER; measures size, granularity, fluorescence; sorts cells; up to 1,000 cells/s.
  • Hayflick limit: normal somatic cells divide only ~40–60 times (telomere shortening). Telomerase (cell lines, germ cells, some stem cells, most cancer cells) bypasses it.
  • Contact inhibition: normal adherent lines stop dividing at confluence; tumor adherent lines keep dividing ⇒ multilayered aggregates.
  • Mature neurons are post-mitotic (G0): Hayflick limit doesn't apply, no line can be made from them directly.

1. Introduction / definitions p. 2

Cell culture The set of laboratory techniques that maintain living cells outside their original organism, where they keep the capacity to proliferate and differentiate in vitro, although they are not organized into tissues.
  • Uses cells, the basic unit of life, as model systems for biological and physiological processes of the human body.
  • Widely used in vaccine development, cancer research and therapeutic protein production, in basic and applied biomedical science.

How to obtain themRelatively easy: a simple blood sample is enoughBiopsy + dissociation steps (below)

Adherent cells: the steps

  1. Biopsy.
  2. Mechanical dissociation: physical disruption of the tissue structure.
  3. Enzymatic dissociation, in order to: eliminate the extracellular matrix, break intercellular junctions, release individual cells.
  4. Debris removal: filtration and washing to remove tissue fragments.
Don't confuse Filtration/washing is debris removal, the last step, not part of enzymatic dissociation. The PDF numbers the three goals of enzymatic dissociation as steps 4–6; they are one step.

How a cell line is obtained p. 3

  1. Start from a primary culture.
  2. Select the desired cell type: sort the cells by flow cytometry and keep only the wanted population.
  3. Immortalize them by introducing viral genes that promote continuous proliferation (genetic modification, most often by viral transduction).
NB — tumor cell lines Tumor cells are by definition resistant to apoptosis (they accumulated multiple mutations enabling uncontrolled proliferation). So a tumor cell line needs only cell sorting, no immortalization step.

· Flow cytometry p. 3 2022-23 · 2023-24 · 2024-25

Flow cytometry A technique that simultaneously measures several physical and biochemical properties of individual cells in suspension.
Parameter measuredNote
1. Relative cell size
2. Internal complexity (granularity)
3. Fluorescence intensitywhen the cell is labeled with a fluorochrome
  1. Cells pass one by one through a LASER beam in the cytometer.
  2. The LASER–cell interaction generates signals, detected and analyzed according to the parameters above.
  3. The cytometer can then sort the cells according to the parameters or fluorescent labels predefined in the connected computer.

Modern cytometers analyze up to 1,000 cells per second: rapid, quantitative single-cell data.

3. Evolution of cells in culture pp. 3–4 2022-23

Lag phase: cells adjust to the new environment→Exponential growth phase: active cell division

Hayflick limit and telomeres

  1. Normal human somatic cells divide only a finite number of times in culture: roughly 40 to 60 divisions = the Hayflick limit. Primary cultures therefore have a limited lifespan.
  2. DNA polymerase cannot fully replicate the ends of linear chromosomes ⇒ the telomeres (protective DNA sequences) shorten at each division.
  3. Critically short telomeres ⇒ DNA-damage responses ⇒ permanent cell-cycle arrest (replicative senescence) ⇒ apoptosis.
Express telomerase (maintain telomeres, bypass the Hayflick limit)Do not
Cell lines · germ cells · certain stem cells · most cancer cellsNormal human somatic cells (primary cultures)

Adherent cell lineAt confluence
NormalProliferates indefinitely but keeps contact inhibition ⇒ stops dividing at confluence
TumorLacks this control ⇒ keeps dividing beyond confluence ⇒ multilayered aggregates
Memory trick Immortal ≠ uncontrolled: a normal line is "immortal but polite" (stops when it touches its neighbours); a tumor line is "immortal and rude" (piles up).

The exception: primary neurons p. 4

  • Mature neurons are terminally differentiated, post-mitotic cells (G0 phase) that no longer divide.
  • The Hayflick limit, which depends on division, does not apply: they survive a limited time in vitro without proliferating, still vulnerable to apoptosis.
  • They cannot divide or be subcultured ⇒ impossible to establish a continuous line directly from healthy adult neurons.
  • Instead: isolate them directly from fresh tissue, or expand neural progenitors and induce them to differentiate into mature neurons.

4–5. Experimental conditions and maintenance p. 4

  • Required: aseptic conditions, appropriate equipment, suitable culture solutions.
  • Change the culture medium every 2–3 days to prevent nutrient depletion.
  • At confluence, cells must be subcultured (passaged) into new flasks.

6. Cell culture and medicine pp. 4–5

A major contribution to medicine: understanding cell physiology led to advances in diagnosis and treatment; it remains a reference, irreplaceable research tool.

6.1 Medical research6.2 Clinical practice
  • Material for basic studies: metabolism, migration, apoptosis…
  • Genomics, transcriptomics, proteomics in personalized medicine
  • Infectious mechanisms and drug development
  • Toxicological testing: drugs are first tested in vitro
  • In vivo studies by implanting cultured cells into animals
  • Stem cell preparation for transplantation, directly or after differentiation
  • Pharmaceutical production: antibodies, hormones, vaccines, insulin…
  • Artificial skin for burn treatment

Exam traps — the wrong options past exams used

2024-25 Q26 — protocol for a line from a malignant breast tumor (key: B)
  • "Biopsy + primary culture + sorting + immortalization by lentiviral transfection" (C) and "biopsy + primary culture + immortalization" (D): false. Tumor cells already resist apoptosis: sorting only. 2024-25
  • "Biopsy + primary culture" (A): false. Without sorting you keep all the tumor's cell types: that is still a primary culture, not a homogeneous line.
2023-24 Q12 — transfection is used to… (key: C, obtain cell lines)
  • "Immortalize tumor cells" (D): false. Tumor cells don't need immortalization. 2023-24
  • "Contaminate cells" (A): false.
  • "Inhibit apoptosis" (B): not in the key. The lesson does say immortalized cells "acquired resistance to apoptosis", so the option is arguable, but the examiner wanted only "obtain cell lines": answer with the purpose of the technique.
2023-24 Q35 — flow cytometry (key: ABC)
  • "Is a cell culture technique" (D): false. It analyses and sorts cells in suspension; it is used to obtain a line, but it does not culture cells. 2023-24
  • ✅ True: reference method for sorting cells from a suspension; multiparametric analysis of many cells one by one; simultaneous measurement of several physical characteristics.
2022-23 Q9 — contact inhibition can be studied on a line of… (key: B, adherent cells at confluence)
  • "Lymphocytes at confluence": false. Lymphocytes are circulating, not adherent; contact inhibition is about adherent lines. 2022-23
  • "Adherent cancer cells at confluence": false. Tumor lines lack contact inhibition (multilayers).
  • "Adherent cells at semi-confluence": false. Inhibition appears at confluence, when cells touch.

Self-check — answer, then tap to reveal

Define cell culture.
Techniques that maintain living cells outside their organism, where they keep proliferating and differentiating in vitro, without being organized into tissues.
How do you obtain lymphocytes? Hepatocytes?
Lymphocytes (circulating): a simple blood sample. Hepatocytes (adherent): biopsy → mechanical dissociation → enzymatic dissociation → debris removal (filtration, washing).
Three goals of enzymatic dissociation?
Eliminate the extracellular matrix, break intercellular junctions, release individual cells.
Primary culture: one advantage, three disadvantages?
Closest to the natural state. But: all cell types mixed, few target cells, short lifespan (senescence → apoptosis).
Protocol for a normal cell line? For a tumor cell line?
Normal: primary culture + flow cytometry sorting + immortalization by viral genes. Tumor: primary culture + sorting only.
What three parameters does flow cytometry measure, and how?
Relative size, granularity, fluorescence intensity; cells in suspension pass one by one through a LASER, up to 1,000 cells/s, and can be sorted.
Two phases after seeding?
Lag phase (adjustment), then exponential growth phase (active division).
Hayflick limit: value and mechanism?
~40–60 divisions; telomeres shorten each division (DNA polymerase can't copy chromosome ends) → DNA-damage response → replicative senescence → apoptosis.
Which cells express telomerase?
Cell lines, germ cells, certain stem cells, most cancer cells.
Normal vs tumor adherent line at confluence?
Normal: contact inhibition, stops dividing (monolayer). Tumor: keeps dividing, multilayered aggregates.
Why can't you make a line from adult neurons? Alternatives?
Post-mitotic (G0), can't divide or be subcultured. Isolate from fresh tissue, or expand neural progenitors and differentiate them.
How often is the medium changed? What at confluence?
Every 2–3 days; passage into new flasks.
Research vs clinical uses: where do toxicology and artificial skin go?
Toxicology (and implanting cells into animals): research. Artificial skin, stem cells for transplantation, pharmaceutical production: clinical practice.
