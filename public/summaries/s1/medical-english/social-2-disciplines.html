1. Anthropology p. 1–4

  • The study of humanity: comparative study of humans as a group from first appearance to the present (Zerihun, 2005). Studies learned and shared strategies for living, shared traits of Homo sapiens, and products of groups — material (tools, clothes, houses) and nonmaterial (beliefs, values, institutions).
  • Also a natural science (man as a product of natural processes, in the animal kingdom). The most holistic of the social sciences.
Sub-fieldFocus

SocioculturalWays of life of ancient and modern peoples; cultural universals, diffusion

Misconceptions Anthropology is not limited to "primitive" societies, rural people or remote exotic communities, and does not aim to preserve defunct cultures in museums.

2. History p. 4–5

  • The study of past events — a systematic attempt to learn about and verify them and relate them to one another and to the present. Historiography = the writing of history based on critical examination of sources.
  • Ibn Khaldun created two sciences at once: historiology and sociology (study of civilization: its beginning, development and decline).
  • Study involves identifying, classifying, arranging, patterning. Fruits: imposition of order, appreciation of variety, possibilities of prediction, realization of limitation.

3. Archaeology p. 5–7

  • Study of the human past through surviving material traces (artifacts, burials, ruins, monuments, inscriptions, coins, seals, pottery…), usually recovered by excavation (e.g. Mohenjo-daro, Harappa, Nalanda in India). Ethno-archaeologists study living people's use of objects.
  • Branches: prehistoric (before writing), historical (remains + texts), industrial (after the Industrial Revolution), classical (Greeks & Romans), Egyptian, Mesoamerican.

4. Geography p. 7–8

  • Study of the natural environment and how it influences social and cultural development. Differs from geology (physical Earth only) because it also studies humans.
  • Greeks first went beyond map-making (cartography). Concerns: ecology, climate, resources, accessibility, demography. Applications: maps, trade patterns, industrial/agricultural decisions, settlement, aggression and acquisition.
  • Two divisions: physical (natural features) and human (human landscape, humans–environment). Link to all disciplines = the spatial perspective.

5. Political science p. 8–9

  • Study of government and politics. Government = the only institution with legal, legitimate authority to use coercive power in a defined area; legitimate = people believe it is "right".
  • Subfields: political theory, comparative politics, international relations, political economy, political sociology, public administration, environmental politics.
  • Evolution: description of institutions (late 19th c.) → political behaviour (mid-20th c.) → relations between actors, institutions and outcomes. Government power reaches "from womb to tomb".

6. Economics p. 10–12

  • Household management (Aristotle) → "study of wealth" (mercantilists, 16th–18th c.) → Smith's self-regulating economy / classical economics (The Wealth of Nations) → Marx (labour theory of value) → Alfred Marshall: a study of wealth and of man.
  • Study of the production and distribution of scarce goods and services; central focus = opportunity cost and optimisation.

Positive economics = facts, "what is?" (e.g. life expectancy in Somalia is rising) · Normative = value judgments, "what ought to be?" (e.g. the poor should pay no taxes).

8. Sociology and Ibn Khaldun p. 13–16

  • Classical theorists: Marx, Spencer, Durkheim, Weber. First book with "Sociology" in its title: Herbert Spencer (mid-19th c.). First US course: University of Kansas, 1890. First European department: Bordeaux, 1895, Durkheim; Germany 1919 (Weber); Poland 1920 (Znaniecki); UK after WWII.
  • Studies social institutions (families, schools), processes (conflict, competition, assimilation, change), problems (crime, poverty), classes, social facts and stratification (unequal distribution of power). Micro-level = small groups, interactions; macro-level = trends in large groups and societies.
  • Ibn Khaldun: 'Ilm al-'Umran (the New Science); introduction = Muqaddimah ("Prologue"); central concept = 'Assabiyya (what holds groups together); dualist typology bedouin / sedentary.
  • Historians' 3 errors: ignorance of the nature of civilization, bias and prejudice, blind acceptance of reports. Remedies: absolute objectivity, checking reports and reporters, not limiting history to politics, wars and rulers.

Name the four main sub-fields of anthropology.

Who is the father of history? Of historiography?
Herodotus · Ibn Khaldun.
Artifacts vs ecofacts?
Material remains of human societies · footprints of past societies on the ecology.
Partial or general equilibrium for microeconomics?
Partial (macro = general).
"Females ought to be given job opportunities" — positive or normative?
Normative (value judgment, "what ought to be").
Structuralism vs functionalism — who and what?
Wundt: structure of consciousness · James: function of behaviour.
Who coined "sociology", when, and from which words?
Auguste Comte, 1838 — Latin socius + Greek logos.
What is Ibn Khaldun's central concept?
'Assabiyya — what holds groups together.
