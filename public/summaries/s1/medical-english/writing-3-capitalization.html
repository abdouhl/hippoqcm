If you remember only this
  • Generic drug names → lowercase (aspirin, metformin); brand names → Capital (Lipitor, Prozac). Prefer the generic name unless there's a clinical reason.
  • Eponyms: capitalize the person's name, not "disease/syndrome": Alzheimer's disease, Down syndrome.
  • Diseases named after places are usually capitalized (Lyme disease, West Nile virus); common diseases are not (pneumonia, cancer).
  • Acronyms: all capitals (MRI, ECG, ICU, GP, NHS).
  • Specialties lowercase ("the cardiology department") unless part of a proper name ("the Department of Cardiology"). Titles capitalized before a name (Dr Smith), not as common nouns ("the doctor").
  • Bacteria: Genus capital, species lowercase, both italic: Staphylococcus aureus → S. aureus.

1–3. Basic rules p. 1–2

  • Medical capitalization differs from general English in some respects (drug names, eponyms, abbreviations).
  • Beginning of a sentence: always a capital.
  • Proper nouns: people, places, days, months, holidays, hospitals, clinics, institutions — "seen at St Mary's Hospital on Monday".
  • The pronoun I is always capitalized, wherever it is.

4. Drug names: generic vs brand p. 2–3

"One of the most important" rules — crucial for accurate prescribing and medication safety.

TypeRuleExamples
Named after people (eponyms)Name capitalized; "disease", "syndrome" lowercaseAlzheimer's disease, Parkinson's disease, Hodgkin's disease, Down syndrome, Tourette syndrome, Zollinger-Ellison syndrome
Named after placesUsually capitalizedLyme disease (Lyme, Connecticut), Middle East Respiratory Syndrome (MERS), West Nile virus
Common diseasesNot capitalized (unless they contain a proper name)arthritis, emphysema, leukemia, migraine, pneumonia, diabetes, hypertension, cancer
Apostrophe in eponyms Some style guides (including SNOMED CT) omit it: "Down syndrome" rather than "Down's syndrome". Check your institution's style.

11. Latin names of microorganisms p. 4–5

  • Binomial (two-part) nomenclature; international convention independent of English rules.
  • Genus capitalized, species lowercase, both italic: Staphylococcus aureus, Escherichia coli.
  • After the first full use the genus may be abbreviated to its initial: S. aureus.
  • Informal, adjectival or plural group use → lowercase, not italic: "the infection was staphylococcal", "a streptococcal throat infection".

12. Headings and document titles p. 5

ConventionRuleExample
Title caseCapitalize all major words (nouns, verbs, adjectives, adverbs); not articles, short prepositions, coordinating conjunctions — unless first wordDischarge Summary for Mrs Alia Thompson
Sentence caseOnly the first word + proper nounsDischarge summary for Mrs Alia Thompson
Writing tip Hospital templates mostly use sentence case for section headings ("Reason for admission", "Medications on discharge") and title case for the document title. Be consistent within one document.

Common errors — from the formative assessment

Fix the capitals
  • "parkinson's disease" → Parkinson's disease · "down syndrome" → Down syndrome · "hodgkin's lymphoma" → Hodgkin's lymphoma
  • "lipitor 20mg" → Lipitor · "aricept" → Aricept (brand) — but metformin, aspirin, ibuprofen, fluoxetine stay lowercase
  • "The mri scan" → MRI · "an ecg and chest x-ray" → ECG, X-ray
  • "st mary's hospital on friday" → St Mary's Hospital on Friday
  • "referred the patient to a cardiologist" — already correct: common noun, no capital.

Self-check — answer, then tap to reveal

Capital or not: ibuprofen, Nurofen, fluconazole, Zyrtec?
ibuprofen (generic) · Nurofen (brand) · fluconazole (generic) · Zyrtec (brand).
Which name should you normally use in formal writing, generic or brand?
Generic — unless there is a specific clinical reason to use the brand.
Write correctly: tourette Syndrome.
Tourette syndrome.
Why is "Lyme disease" capitalized but "pneumonia" not?
Lyme is a place name; common diseases without a proper name are lowercase.
"referred to the cardiology department" vs "the Department of Cardiology" — why the difference?
Common noun vs proper department name.
"The Doctor examined the patient" — correct?
No: "the doctor" (common noun). Capital only before a name: Dr Smith.
How is Escherichia coli written, and after first use?
Genus capital, species lowercase, both italic; later E. coli.
Italic or not: "a streptococcal throat infection"?
Not italic, lowercase (adjectival form).
Title case or sentence case for section headings in most hospital templates?
Sentence case ("Reason for admission"); title case for the document title.
