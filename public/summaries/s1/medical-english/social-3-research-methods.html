If you remember only this
  • Origins: ancient Greeks (6th–5th c. BC); a distinct form of research only in the 18th century (Enlightenment, Industrial & French revolutions), pushed by positivism (Auguste Comte, A General View of Positivism, 1848).
  • Ontology = how we see the world; epistemology = the best way to study it (Burrell & Morgan, 1979). Statistics entered social science with Durkheim's Suicide (1897).
  • Inductive = theory-building (data → theory); deductive = theory-testing (theory → new data). Two halves of a cycle — better seen as a helix.
  • "Paradigm" popularized by Thomas Kuhn (1962). 5 paradigms: positivism (objectivity, deductive logic, value-free) · interpretivism (social actors) · social constructionism (truth socially constructed) · critical (power, inequality, social change) · postmodernism (no knowable absolute truth).
  • 4 types of social research: qualitative / quantitative, primary / secondary. Researcher attitudes: curiosity, scepticism, objectivity.

1. Historical roots p. 1–2

  • Several social sciences — anthropology, sociology, economics, psychology, political science, history — each with its own concepts, methods and data, but all interested in human behaviour.
  • McDonald (1993): by the 6th century BC, notions of social convention, causal relations and hypothetical knowledge had been formulated; by the 5th century BC a sophisticated understanding of methodological issues.
  • 18th century: social philosophy ("philosophical attitude") gave way to a scientific emphasis. Positivism = knowledge based on actual positive sense experience; it moved away from metaphysical speculation toward facts and falsifiable statements.
  • Burrell & Morgan, Sociological Paradigms and Organizational Analysis (1979): two sets of assumptions — ontology (social order or constant change?) and epistemology (objective or subjective approach?).

2. Social research: inductive and deductive p. 2

Scientific knowledge = systematically gathered, classified, related and interpreted; two major fields: social and natural science. Natural sciences are precise, accurate, deterministic and independent of the observer; social sciences are less accurate, deterministic or unambiguous.

InductiveDeductive
GoalInfer concepts and patterns from observed dataTest concepts and patterns from theory using new empirical data
Other nameTheory-buildingTheory-testing (also refine, improve, extend)
Most valuable whenFew prior theoriesMany competing theories — which works best?

A complete researcher can traverse the whole cycle. "Elegant theories are not valuable if they do not match with reality"; "mountains of data are useless" until they build meaningful theories.

3. Social research paradigms p. 3–4

Paradigm (Kuhn, The Structure of Scientific Revolutions, 1962) = a way of viewing the world; a way of framing what we know, what we can know, and how we can know it.

ParadigmEmphasisAssumption
PositivismObjectivity, knowability, deductive logicSociety can and should be studied empirically and scientifically; value-free sociology
InterpretivismResearch on humans (people, not objects) — Saunders, Lewis & Thornhill (2009)People interpret their social roles (theatre analogy) and give meaning to them and to others' roles
Social constructionismTruth as varying, socially constructed, ever-changingReality is created collectively; social context and interaction frame it (e.g. hand gestures differ across regions)
CriticalPower, inequality, social changeSocial science can never be value-free; research should aim at social change
PostmodernismInherent problems with previous paradigmsTruth may or may not be knowable; "whose power, whose truth?" (Aylesworth, 2015)
Trap Social constructionism is not only individualistic: groups (a couple, a nation) agree on what is true, and meanings have power beyond the individuals who create them.

4. The scientific method p. 5–6

A standardized set of techniques to build scientific knowledge (valid observations, interpretation, generalization). Cycle: develop a hypothesis → test → modify → retest until consistent with observations → broad explanations = scientific theories. Hypothesis = "a tentative statement about a relationship between observable facts or events". Social problems (racism, poverty, crime…) are interdisciplinary (Harrison).

  1. Observing the real world.
  2. Defining the problem.
  3. Reviewing the literature (background, approaches, what is already covered).
  4. Observing some more (sharper observation).
  5. Developing a theoretical framework and formulating a hypothesis.
  6. Choosing a research design (survey, experiment, observational study, existing sources) — "the crux of the research process".
  7. Collecting the data — conclusions are only as good as the data.
  8. Analysing the results → hypothesis confirmed, rejected or modified.
  9. Drawing conclusions (report).

5. Challenges of social research p. 7–8

  • Facts: similar but each unique → hard to classify; generalizations less definite.
  • Laws: not measurable (satisfaction, democracy); society extremely complex; impossible to find all causes; the human element.
  • Control: controlled experiments seldom used — careful observation and abstraction instead.
  • Competence in providing theories: more needed than in physical sciences (where experiment proves true/wrong).
  • Objectivity: researchers are human — likes, prejudices, biases.

Who articulated positivism, and in which work?
Auguste Comte, A General View of Positivism (1848).
Ontology vs epistemology?
How we see the world · the best way to study it.
Which work first brought statistical analysis into social science?
Durkheim's Suicide (1897).
Which research is theory-building, and when is it most valuable?
Inductive · when there are few prior theories.
Which paradigm says social science can never be value-free and aims at social change?
The critical paradigm.
Name the 4 quantitative research methods.
Survey, correlational, causal-comparative, experimental.
What is "blind data mining"?
Collecting data first with available instruments and only then deciding what to do with it.
