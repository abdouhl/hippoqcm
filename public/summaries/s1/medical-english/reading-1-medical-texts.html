If you remember only this
  • Primary literature = original research articles (new discoveries, trial results, case studies). Secondary = systematic reviews, meta-analyses, narrative reviews.
  • Peer-reviewed journals are more reliable; search with PubMed (National Library of Medicine).
  • Research article = IMRAD (Introduction, Methods, Results, Discussion) + structured abstract.
  • Read efficiently: abstract conclusions first (relevance) → methods and results; at each step decide whether to go on.
  • Case report = detailed narrative of one patient (rarely up to four); the case presentation is its heart; requires written informed consent and de-identification.

1–4. What medical texts are, and their types p. 1–3

Specialized written works in medicine (from ancient treatises to research articles, records, guidelines) for healthcare, education and research; defined by specialized terminology, structured formats, accuracy and clarity.

CategoryWhatExamples
Primary literatureOriginal research: discoveries, clinical trials, case studies — foundation of medical scienceOriginal research article
Secondary textsSynthesize primary researchSystematic reviews, meta-analyses, narrative reviews
Manuals & textbooksDisorders, treatments, management, prognosisCecil Textbook of Medicine, Harrison's Principles of Internal Medicine
Patient records & reportsConfidential: histories, diagnoses, plans, outcomes—
Educational materialsFormal education and patient awarenessLecture notes, patient education brochures, digital health info
Historical treatisesEvolution of medical thoughtEdwin Smith Papyrus (Egypt), Sushruta Samhita (India), The Canon of Medicine (Ibn Sina)
  • Harrison's = the most widely read medical textbook, organized by region and symptom. Canon of Medicine (Ibn Sina) = medieval encyclopedia integrating Greek, Roman and Islamic medicine.
  • Roles: academic teaching, clinical practice (evidence-based care, continuity), research advancement, interdisciplinary collaboration.

5. Why read medical texts p. 3–4

Reading is still the principal way adults acquire new knowledge; you can't read everything in depth → choose topics you already know something about; read the abstract's conclusions first, then methods and results (abstract, then body), appraising at each step.

8 reasons to read journal articles (after Sackett & Durbin)
1 Stay updated · 2 Solve (clinical) problems · 3 Understand disease (causes, features, progression) · 4 Grasp fundamentals (pathophysiology) · 5 Generate research ideas · 6 Academic requirements · 7 Support own views · 8 Impress others

6.1–6.2 Journal articles p. 5

  • Original research article → IMRAD preceded by a structured abstract; designs: RCTs, controlled clinical trials (CCTs), experiments, surveys, cohort, case-control studies.
Review typeFeatures
Narrative reviewQualitative overview of a broad topic, no specific research question
Systematic reviewSpecific question, defined methods for selecting papers, rigorous evaluation of studies
Meta-analysisGoes further: combines statistical findings of several studies to answer a question quantitatively

6.3 Step-by-step reading of a research paper p. 5–7

  1. Title — first point of attraction; best titles are descriptive and self-explanatory ("Comparison of Microwave with Conventional Tissue Processing…" beats "Microwave processing – A blessing for pathologists").
  2. Abstract — screening tool to decide relevance; often free online; structured (Introduction, Methods, Results, Conclusions), word-limited. Check: what, why, how, main findings, meaning.
  3. Introduction — justifies the study: broad background → literature → gap; states aims (usually at its end) and often the hypothesis (check later in the discussion).
  4. Research question — from background + hypothesis.
  5. Methods — design, sample, data collection, statistics → judges validity.
  6. Results — key findings, trends, significance (p-values, confidence intervals), tables and figures.
  7. Discussion & conclusion — interpretation, implications, limitations, future research; are claims supported by data?
  8. References & study design — reliability and strength of evidence.

7. Case reports p. 7–11

Detailed narrative of a patient's presentation, history, diagnosis, treatment, outcomes and follow-up — often an unusual condition; a rapid way to share insights missed by large trials.

SectionContent
AbstractKey aspects and lessons, 150–250 words
IntroductionProblem, relevance, what makes the case unique; brief literature review
Case presentation — the heartBackground & complaints, medical/family history, findings & tests, interventions, follow-up; chronological, with tables/timelines
DiscussionLinks to literature, differential diagnosis, management choices, novelty, implications
Conclusion (sometimes)Take-home messages
References, keywords, acknowledgments—
  • Educational value: real-life scenarios, rare/new conditions (adverse reactions, novel interventions), clinical reasoning, foundation for future research (many discoveries start as case reports).
  • Ethics: written informed consent (patient or guardian) + de-identification; transparent, accurate reporting.
  • What deserves a case report (ICMJE): rare/unique presentation · innovative diagnosis or therapy · atypical progression/outcome · ethical or public-health relevance · educational merit (even common conditions).
  • Critical analysis: question the reasoning and strategies; use checklists such as JBI and CARE guidelines.

8–9. Research article vs case report p. 11–13

FeatureResearch articleCase report
PurposeBroad questions, generalizable dataDocument unique/rare cases
StructureIMRADNarrative: introduction, case presentation, discussion
SampleLarge populationsSingle patient (rarely up to four)
MethodRigorous, statistics, controlsObservational, descriptive, minimal statistics
OutcomeStatistical significance, hypothesis testingEducational value, hypothesis generation
Peer reviewStandardStandard
LimitationsMay miss rare/novel eventsNot generalizable, anecdotal
ReferencesExtensiveSelective (highlight novelty)

Case reports also contribute to pharmacovigilance. Tips: prioritize papers on your topic, decide at each section whether to continue, learn basic biostatistics, compare several papers on a topic.

Self-check — answer, then tap to reveal

Is a systematic review primary or secondary literature?
Secondary (it synthesizes primary research).
Main purpose of reading the abstract?
To quickly decide if the article is relevant to your interests.
Which section discusses implications and limitations?
The Discussion.
Narrative vs systematic review vs meta-analysis?
Broad qualitative overview · specific question with defined methods · statistical combination of studies.
True or false: case reports are based on large samples.
False — one patient (rarely up to four).
Give 3 features that make a case worth reporting.
Rare/unique presentation; innovative diagnosis/treatment; atypical course/outcome (also ethical/public-health relevance, educational merit).
"We conducted a double-blind, placebo-controlled trial…" — which genre?
Research article. "A 32-year-old woman presented with…" → case report.
