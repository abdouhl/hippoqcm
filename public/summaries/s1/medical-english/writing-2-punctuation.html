If you remember only this
  • Always use the serial (Oxford) comma before "and/or" in lists of 3+: "diabetes, hypertension, and heart disease".
  • Comma splice = two independent clauses joined by a comma only. Fix with ; · , + FANBOYS · .
  • Semicolon: joins two complete, related clauses without a conjunction; goes before a transition (; however,); separates list items that already contain commas.
  • Colon: introduces a list, explanation, quotation; time (08:30), ratios (1:2), title: subtitle. It must follow a complete clause — never right after a verb ("were:") or a preposition ("with:").
  • Periods in a.m., p.m., e.g., i.e., vs., Dr., et al. — not in acronyms (WHO, MRI, ICU, ER, ECG). Decimal point: 0.5 mg, not 0,5.
  • Apostrophe: patient's (one) · patients' (several) · children's; no contractions in formal writing; no apostrophe for plain plurals.

1. Why punctuation matters in healthcare p. 1–2

  • 5 reasons: accuracy (a misplaced comma alters a diagnosis/order), patient safety (ambiguity → medication errors), professional credibility, legal protection (clear documentation protects providers in litigation), standardisation (multidisciplinary teams).
  • Error → consequence: missing comma → dosing error; incorrect colon → diagnosis misinterpretation; missing period → treatment confusion ("Do not administer. Aspirin if patient has fever"); inconsistent list → incomplete documentation.

2. The period (full stop) p. 2–4

The most fundamental mark: ends declarative and imperative sentences, marks abbreviations, separates sentences.

RuleExamples
1. End every complete sentenceAdminister 10 mg of morphine sulphate.
2. Periods in common abbreviationsa.m. (ante meridiem), p.m. (post meridiem), e.g. (exempli gratia), i.e. (id est), vs. (versus), Dr., Mr./Ms./Mrs., et al. (and others)
3. No periods in acronymsWHO (not W.H.O.), MRI, ICU, ER, ECG
4. Decimal points0.5 mg (not 0,5 mg), 37.5°C, 7.2 mmol/L

Errors: missing period (two sentences run together) and unnecessary period ("were stable., and…").

3. The comma p. 4–8

A brief pause within a sentence.

RuleExample
1. Items in a list — serial/Oxford comma strongly recommendedfever, chills, and headache · amoxicillin, cephalexin, or ciprofloxacin
2. Before FANBOYS joining two independent clausesThe diagnosis was uncertain, so additional tests were ordered.
3. After introductory words/phrases/clausesHowever, … · After the surgery, … · To confirm the diagnosis, …
4. Around non-essential informationThe patient, who had a history of diabetes, presented with foot pain. · Dr. Chen, the chief surgeon, …
5. Dates and addressesSeptember 15, 2024 · 123 Medical Drive, Suite 300, Boston, MA 02115
6. Between coordinate adjectivesa severe, persistent cough — test: can you put "and" between them?
7. Direct addressMr. Smith, your test results are normal.
8. After transitional phrasesTherefore, · Furthermore, · In conclusion, · For example,
Essential (restrictive) = no commas "Patients who have diabetes should monitor their blood sugar." — the clause identifies which patients.
Why the serial comma matters "diabetes, hypertension and heart disease" could suggest the last two are related; with the comma → three separate conditions.

4. The semicolon p. 8–11

Pause stronger than a comma, less final than a period.

  1. Joins two related independent clauses without a conjunction — both must be able to stand alone: "The ECG showed ST-segment elevation; this indicates possible myocardial infarction." ✗ "The patient presented with; fever and chills."
  2. Before a transitional expression (conjunctive adverb) joining two clauses — semicolon before, comma after: "The diagnosis was confirmed; therefore, treatment was initiated immediately."
  3. Separates items in a complex list (items containing commas): "Boston, Massachusetts; Los Angeles, California; and Chicago, Illinois."
  4. Clarifies and organises complex information (past medical history with dates and details).
Transition categoryExamples
Additionmoreover, furthermore, in addition
Contrasthowever, nevertheless, on the other hand
Cause/effecttherefore, consequently, as a result
Timesubsequently, meanwhile, later
Emphasisindeed, in fact, certainly

Errors: semicolon where a colon is needed ("The diagnosis was; …"), semicolon after a dependent clause ("Because …; we ordered" → comma), comma where a semicolon is needed ("discharged, however, …" → "; however,"), overuse.

5. The colon p. 11–14

UseExample
1. Introduce a list (after a complete clause)The following tests were ordered: complete blood count, urinalysis, and chest X-ray.
2. Explanation / elaborationThe diagnosis was clear: the patient had a myocardial infarction.
3. Formal quotationThe surgeon stated: "We will proceed with the procedure as planned."
4. Time and ratios08:30 · ratio 1:2
5. Title and subtitleDiagnostic Imaging: Principles and Practice
  • After a colon: lowercase if a clause, phrase or list follows (most common); capital if a complete sentence follows (more formal). Choose one style and be consistent.
  • Errors: colon after a verb ("symptoms were: …" → "were the following: …"), after a preposition ("treated with: …"), colon instead of semicolon between two clauses.
The PDF contradicts itself On p. 12 "The patient's symptoms included: fever, chills…" is shown both as correct and as incorrect ("but the colon is still acceptable here"); p. 18 calls the colon after "includes" an error. Safest answer: add "the following" before the colon.

6. Lists of symptoms, dosages, instructions p. 14–16

  • Symptoms: in-text list (commas) · vertical list (colon + bullets) · complex list (semicolons, when items have "which…").
  • Dosages: commas = clear for simple lists; semicolons + commas = clear for complex items (drug, dose, indication); colon + semicolons = very clear.
  • Instructions: numbers for sequential steps (check ID → verify order → administer → document); bullets for non-sequential items.
  • Best practices: be consistent, use parallel structure, semicolons for lists with internal commas.

7–9. Records, case studies, research p. 16–20

  • Physical exam notes: heading + colon, findings separated by commas, groups by semicolons — "Cardiovascular: Regular rate and rhythm; no murmurs, rubs, or gallops."
  • Case studies (p. 17–19): missing comma ("fever headache and nausea"), colon after "includes", comma splice before "however", missing serial comma, missing period between two instructions, comma splice ("chest pain, the ECG was ordered" → ; / , so / .).
  • Abstracts: periods end sentences, commas in lists, semicolons join related sentences, colons introduce findings (Background: / Methods: / Results: / Conclusion:). Journals require correct punctuation; it affects readability and acceptance.

Common errors — the lecture's "mistakes to avoid" list

Spot and fix
  • Comma splice: "The patient presented with fever, he also reported chills." → ; / , and / .
  • Missing serial comma: "lisinopril, metformin and aspirin" → "metformin, and aspirin".
  • Colon after a verb / preposition: "were: …", "treated with: …".
  • Comma before "however" joining clauses → "; however,".
  • Unnecessary comma between subject and verb: "The patient, was discharged".
  • Missing commas around non-essential info: "The patient, who had a history of heart disease, was readmitted."
  • Formative: "The patients chart" → patient's; "January 1 2020" → "January 1, 2020"; "If the condition worsens, call…"; "The doctor said, "Please describe your symptoms.""

Self-check — answer, then tap to reveal

Give the 5 reasons punctuation matters in healthcare.
Accuracy, patient safety, professional credibility, legal protection, standardisation.
Periods or not: e.g., WHO, a.m., ICU, et al.?
e.g. ✓ · WHO ✗ · a.m. ✓ · ICU ✗ · et al. ✓ (acronyms take no periods).
Three ways to fix "The patient was experiencing chest pain, the ECG was ordered."
"…pain; the ECG…" · "…pain, so the ECG…" · "…pain. The ECG…"
Commas or not: "Patients who have diabetes should monitor their blood sugar."
No commas — the clause is essential (it identifies which patients).
Test for coordinate adjectives?
If you can put "and" between them, use a comma: "a severe, persistent cough".
Punctuate: "The medication was effective however the patient experienced mild nausea."
"The medication was effective; however, the patient experienced mild nausea."
When do list items take semicolons?
When one or more items already contain commas (complex list).
What's wrong with "The patient was treated with: antibiotics and antipyretics."?
A colon after a preposition; remove it.
Numbers or bullets for nursing steps that must happen in order?
Numbers (sequential); bullets for non-sequential items.
the patient's / the patients' / the children's — which is plural possession of a regular noun?
the patients' (several patients).
Hyphen or en dash: "a 10_15 mg dose"? "a 45_year_old man"?
En dash for the range (10–15); hyphens for the compound modifier (45-year-old).
Parentheses or square brackets for an editor's insertion in a quotation?
Square brackets: "The patient [Mr Adams] was transferred…"
