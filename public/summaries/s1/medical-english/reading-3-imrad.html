If you remember only this
  • IMRaD (pronounced "im-rad") = Introduction, Methods, Results and Discussion — the most common structure of scientific/medical papers; a universal template for writers and readers.
  • Each section answers one question: Introduction → Why was it done? · Methods → How? · Results → What was found? · Discussion → What does it mean?
  • Results = data only, no interpretation (tables, graphs, statistics). Interpretation, limitations and implications belong to the Discussion.
  • Methods must be replicable: design (RCT, cohort…), participants, materials, procedures.
  • Mapping = breaking an article into its IMRaD sections to extract essentials; tools: tables, mind maps, flowcharts, digital templates.

1–2. What is IMRaD and why is it used? p. 1–2

  • An acronym from the four main sections of a paper; like a "universal template or map" (Cusen, 2018; Moskovitz et al., 2023).
  • Logical sequence: question → methods used to investigate it → results → implications drawn from the conclusions.
  • Quick access: readers find what they need without reading the whole paper — a statistician goes straight to the Methods, a clinician jumps to Results and Discussion.
  • It is the structure of many journals → a universal standard in research communication ("a common recipe format that all cooks understand"; "the essential storytelling framework of scientific research").

Table 2.1 The IMRaD format explained p. 2

SectionQuestion it answersKey content
IntroductionWhy was this study done?Background context & existing knowledge · the specific gap in knowledge · the clear research question or hypothesis
MethodsHow was the study done?The detailed "recipe" · study design (e.g. RCT, cohort) · participants, materials, procedures · must be replicable by others
ResultsWhat did you find?Objective presentation of all data · key results in tables, graphs, charts · statistical analysis · no interpretation
DiscussionWhat do the findings mean?Interpretation · fit with existing knowledge · limitations · implications for future work or practice
Trap "Our results suggest that…" or "This is the first study to show…" is Discussion (interpretation), not Results. Sample size and procedures are Methods.

3–4. Mapping a research article p. 3–4

A structured method for reading and summarizing: break the article into its IMRaD sections to extract essential information. Why map? It improves understanding and recall · identifies the research question, methods and key results · supports critical evaluation of quality and relevance · prepares readers to apply findings.

SectionQuestions to ask
IntroductionWhat problem does the study address? What is the research question or goal?
MethodsHow was it conducted? What design? Who participated, what intervention? How were data collected?
ResultsWhat were the main findings? What changes or outcomes were observed?
DiscussionWhat do the results mean? How do they relate to broader goals? Limitations? Future actions recommended?

Tools for mapping: tables, mind maps, flowcharts, digital templates. Mapping turns reading into an active learning process.

Table 4.1 Research map: "Hand Washing Training for Posyandu Cadres" (Susila et al., 2025) p. 4–6

SectionKey elements
IProblem: diarrhea and typhoid in Indonesian children (bacteria: Salmonella, E. coli; poor hygiene) · diarrhea = 31% of deaths under 1 year, 25% at 1–4 years (2018 Riskesdas); 100,000 children/year die (WHO) · cause: lack of awareness, not washing hands before eating · solution: hand washing with soap (CTPS) within Clean and Healthy Living Behavior (PHBS) · type: community service activity
MEducational intervention · 10 Posyandu cadres (community health volunteers) · counseling, PowerPoint, printed brochures · procedure: material → group discussion & Q&A → practical demonstration · evaluation: correct demonstration after training, observation of behavior
RAll 10 attended · all could understand the importance and correctly demonstrate CTPS · improved knowledge and practice, active participation
DTraining worked; cadres become health educators · hand washing = simple, low-cost prevention · WHO: soap hand washing reduces diarrhea risk by 40% and respiratory infections by 20% · implied limitations: small sample (10), no pre/post-tests or long-term follow-up · recommendation: expand training, integrate CTPS into regular programs

One-line summary: Problem (child diarrhea) → Intervention (training cadres) → Result (improved knowledge & skills) → Impact (cadres promote hygiene).

Excerpt "Respiratory infections are a leading cause of school absenteeism. This study aimed to evaluate whether daily hand hygiene education reduces illness in elementary schools. We conducted a randomized controlled trial in 10 schools (n=1,200 students) over 12 weeks… The primary outcome was number of sick days reported. Results showed a 30% reduction in absences due to respiratory illness in the intervention group (p<0.01). Our findings support integrating hygiene education into school health programs, though long-term effects require further study."
