If you remember only this
  • Inequality = measurable differences (may be natural, e.g. age) · inequity = differences that are avoidable, unfair and unjust.
  • 5 dimensions of access: geographic, financial, cultural, timely, quality.
  • Inverse Care Law (Julian Tudor Hart, 1971): "the availability of good medical care tends to vary inversely with the need for it" — strongest where care is exposed to market forces.
  • Key numbers: 33 years life-expectancy gap · $17/capita health spending in low-income countries · medicine stockouts 41% vs 4% · COVID vaccine by end 2021: 64.1% vs 5.4%.
  • Equity principles: justice, solidarity, accountability, participation. Algeria: 5.2% of GDP, life expectancy 76 (2024), El-Chifa card.

1. Defining inequality in health access p. 1–2

Health inequalityHealth inequity
Measurable differences in outcomes or access between groups; may come from natural variation, personal choices… (older vs younger adults = inequality, not injustice)Differences that are avoidable, unfair, unjust, stemming from unfair systems, policies and practices — we could and should change them

WHO: equity in health = "minimizing avoidable disparities in health and its determinants… between groups with different underlying social attributes (income, gender, ethnicity, geography…)". Access barriers become inequities when they systematically affect certain groups (the poor, minorities, women, migrants, Indigenous peoples, people with disabilities).

2. The global picture p. 2–3

  • Between countries: people in the lowest life-expectancy countries live 33 years less.
  • Within countries: Canada's universal care lowers financial barriers, but low-income and Indigenous communities face gaps in dental care, pharmacare, mental health. Specialist-access difficulty linked to income < $50,000, rural residence, no family physician (Canadian Longitudinal Study on Aging).
  • LMICs: $17 per capita/year on health; by 2030 >80% of low-income countries projected to spend less than in 2024; greatest intraregional inequalities in Asia and Africa (maternal & child health); NCD medicine stockouts 41% of low-income vs 4% of high-income countries.
  • COVID-19 vaccine divide: end 2021, ≥1 dose in 64.1% (high-income) vs 5.4% (low-income); "vaccine nationalism" reflected policy choices.

3. Barriers to health access p. 3–4

BarrierMain elements
FinancialDirect (consultations, medicines, hospital fees) · indirect (transport, lost wages) · hidden (healthy food, lifestyle)
GeographicLong distances · no affordable transport · few professionals in rural areas · poor roads
Social & culturalLanguage · discrimination, misunderstandings · immigration difficulties · stigma (HIV, mental disorders)
Systemic & structuralStaff shortage · poor coordination · inadequate information systems · weak governance

Intersection: barriers overlap and reinforce each other (e.g. a poor rural woman from an ethnic minority). Underlying social determinants: poverty, education, employment, gender, ethnicity.

4. Consequences p. 4–5

  • Mortality & disease: maternal and child mortality, complications of chronic disease, avoidable deaths (diabetes, hypertension, cancer).
  • Economic & social: poverty trap (illness ↔ poverty), lost productivity, intergenerational effects.
  • Pressure on systems: more emergency use, higher costs, less prevention.
  • Loss of trust → people avoid seeking care.

Health equity = a fair opportunity for everyone to achieve good health according to their needs; 4 principles: justice (fair distribution), solidarity (shared responsibility), accountability (monitoring systems), participation (involving communities).

7. The Algerian healthcare system p. 6–8

  • Dual system: public (Ministry of Health) + growing private sector; 5.2% of GDP.
  • Progress: life expectancy 76 years (2024); infant mortality 18.7/1,000; maternal mortality 117.4 → 48.5/100,000 (1999–2019).
  • Boussemghoun study (2025), 400 adults: 39.7% ≥1 chronic disease · 36.5% travelled to other regions · 52.3% no GP visit in the past year.
  • Use higher with income, in women and widowed people, with several chronic medications; limited by travel time. Free public care but indirect costs (transport, lost wages, accommodation).
  • El-Chifa card: insurance, full coverage of some chronic-disease prescriptions — but insurance alone does not guarantee access.
  • Responses: bigger budget (2025 Finance Law), Second National Cancer Plan (2025–2030), Ministry of Pharmaceutical Industry, rural screening. Recommended: rural specialists, telehealth, health education, mobile services for nomads, community health workers.
