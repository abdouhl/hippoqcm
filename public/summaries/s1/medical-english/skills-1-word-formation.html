If you remember only this
  • Medical vocabulary = a rule-governed morphological system (Greek & Latin) with morphological transparency → learn by analysis, not rote memorization.
  • 4 components: root = semantic core · prefix = semantic modifier · combining vowel (usually o) = no meaning, eases pronunciation · suffix = diagnostic/procedural indicator, often the most significant part.
  • Greek → diagnostic, pathological, functional terms; Latin → anatomical, descriptive terms.
  • 4 processes: compounding (osteoarthritis), affixation (hypertension, dermatitis), blending of roots (gastroenterology), derivation (cardiology → cardiologist → cardiac → cardiopathy).
  • Decoding order: suffix → prefix → root(s) → reconstruct meaning.

1–2. The four components of a medical term p. 2–4

ComponentRoleExamples
Root — semantic core / anchorEssential meaning (organ, tissue, system, pathology); stable across a word familycardi heart · nephr kidney · neur nerve · dermat skin · hemat blood · oste bone · glyc sugar
Prefix — semantic modifierDirection, quantity, location, intensity, time; often tells if a state is normal, excessive, deficient or abnormal — a core diagnostic indicatorhyper- excessive · hypo- deficient · tachy- fast · brady- slow · dys- abnormal/impaired · a-/an- absence · peri- surrounding
Combining vowel — morphophonemic connectorNo meaning; eases pronunciation, avoids consonant clusters, links roots/suffixescardi/o/logy · neur/o/logy · gastr/o/enter/o/logy
Suffix — diagnostic & procedural indicatorGrammatical and clinical category: disease, procedure, specialty; often the most diagnostically significant part-itis inflammation · -osis abnormal condition · -emia blood condition · -ectomy surgical removal · -scopy visual examination · -logy field of study · -pathy disease

hyperglycemia vs hypoglycemia, tachycardia vs bradycardia: opposite conditions distinguished only by the prefix.

3. Greek and Latin foundations p. 4–5

OriginMainly used forLecture's example
GreekDiagnostic, pathological, functional termscardiology (scientific discipline)
LatinAnatomical structures, descriptive termscardiac (adjective in descriptive anatomy)

The dual system creates semantic layering: parallel forms for related concepts coexist. Etymology is a strategic decoding tool, not just history.

4. Word formation processes p. 5–6

Medical English is productive: new terms are formed logically from existing morphemes.

ProcessDefinitionExample
CompoundingTwo or more roots → one termosteoarthritis = osteo (bone) + arthritis (joint inflammation)
AffixationAdd prefixes/suffixes to a root — one of the most productive processeshyper + tension ("abnormally high pressure") · dermat + itis
Blending of rootsSeveral combining forms in one term (systems rather than single organs)gastr (stomach) + enter (intestine) + -logy
DerivationNew words from one root → a lexical familycardiology (field) → cardiologist (specialist) → cardiac (adjective) → cardiopathy (disease)

5. The decoding model p. 6–8

  1. Suffix — most stable marker; gives the diagnostic frame (disease, procedure, condition, discipline).
  2. Prefix — intensity (hyper-, hypo-), location (endo-, intra-, sub-), negation (a-, anti-), time/function (pre-, post-).
  3. Root(s) — core anatomical, physiological or biochemical concept (several in compounds).
  4. Reconstruct meaning — synthesis + contextual reasoning, not mechanical.
Worked example hyper- (excessive) + glyc- (glucose) + -emia (blood condition) = abnormally high concentration of glucose in the blood. Transfer: hyperglycemia → hypoglycemia → glycemia.

6–8. Lexical families, irregularities, study implications p. 8–9

  • Lexical family neur-: neurology, neurologist, neuralgia, neuropathy, neurodegeneration → better retention, association, retrieval. Vocabulary learning is network-based, not linear.
  • Irregularities come from historical borrowing, clinical standardization, US/UK variation, abbreviations: myocardial infarction = "heart attack" (clinical synonymy); ECG vs EKG (regional variation).
  • Study strategies: analytical decomposition, spaced repetition of lexical families, authentic clinical texts, morphological mapping, active retrieval.
