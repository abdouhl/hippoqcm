If you remember only this
  • 8 parts of speech; this lecture studies 7 (interjections are rare in formal medical writing).
  • Content words (noun, verb, adjective, adverb) = main meaning, open class (new terms coined constantly). Function words (pronoun, preposition, conjunction) = organise/connect, closed class → must be memorised.
  • Same root, different class: diagnose (V) → diagnosis (N) → diagnostic (Adj). Wrong form = ungrammatical sentence.
  • Nouns from verbs with -ment, -ion, -ing (gerund): treat → treatment, examine → examination.
  • Nouns as adjectives: heart failure, tissue damage, cancer treatment.
  • Conjunctions: coordinating (equal elements), subordinating (dependent → independent clause), correlative pairs (both…and, either…or, neither…nor, not only…but also).

1. Why parts of speech matter p. 2–3

  • Building-block metaphor: the body = cells, tissues, organs; a sentence = words organised into grammatical categories.
  • A word's class tells us where it goes in a sentence, how it changes form, and which words it combines with.
  • Later lectures (sentence building, tense, articles, modality) all depend on this "toolbox"/metalanguage.

Classesnouns, verbs, adjectives, adverbspronouns, prepositions, conjunctions

2. Nouns p. 3–4

Name a person, place, thing, substance, condition or idea; function as subjects, objects or complements. Very dense in medicine (patient, stethoscope, abdomen, fracture).

TypeDefinitionExamples
CommonGeneral namespatient, diagnosis, medication, ward
ProperSpecific names (capitalised)Royal Melbourne Hospital, Dr. Patterson
AbstractIdeas, conditions, qualitiespain, anxiety, compliance, deterioration, hypertension
CompoundTwo or more words = one nounblood pressure, discharge summary, follow-up appointment, healthcare
  • Countable (one symptom, two symptoms) vs uncountable (blood, oxygen, pain) — affects articles and quantifiers.
  • Nouns from verbs (-ment, -ion, -ing): examine → examination · treat → treatment · administer → administration · evaluate → evaluation.

3. Pronouns p. 4–5

Replace a noun or noun phrase to avoid repetition and improve flow.

TypeFormsExample
SubjectI, you, he, she, it, we, they"She presented with chest pain."
Objectme, you, him, her, it, us, them"The consultant reviewed him."
Possessive (pronouns/determiners)my, your, his, her, its, our, their"His physiotherapist recommends…"
Demonstrativesthis, that, these, those"This symptom appeared yesterday."

Tip: once name and gender are established, use he or she consistently.

4. Verbs p. 5–6

Express an action, occurrence or state — "the engine of the sentence".

TypeRoleExamples
ActionPhysical or mental actionsexamine, diagnose, prescribe, administer, refer, discharge, monitor
StateConditions or statespresent (with), suffer (from), require, indicate, demonstrate
LinkingConnect subject to a complementappear, remain, seem, become, feel
Tense in clinical documentationExample
Past — past history"Mr Thomas was diagnosed with Type 2 diabetes in 2019."
Present perfect"She has been prescribed metformin 500 mg twice daily."
Present — current status"The patient remains stable at the time of writing."

Tip: use the passive in formal writing to sound objective and professional.

5. Adjectives p. 6–7

Describe/modify a noun or pronoun: quality, quantity, size, severity, state — communicate severity and urgency.

CategoryExamples
Severitysevere, mild, moderate, acute, chronic, persistent, recurring
Clinical statestable, deteriorating, improving, responsive, non-compliant, elevated, swollen, tender
Patient descriptorselderly, mobile, independent, ambulatory
Nouns used as adjectives tissue damage · heart failure · cancer treatment — the first noun modifies the second.

6. Adverbs p. 7

Modify a verb, adjective or adverb — answer how, when, to what degree. Onset can carry diagnostic significance: gradually vs suddenly.

CategoryExamples
Frequencyregularly, occasionally, intermittently, frequently, rarely
Degreesignificantly, mildly, moderately, markedly
Time / mannercurrently, previously, recently, suddenly, gradually, steadily

7. Prepositions p. 7–8

Show the relationship between a noun/pronoun and another word: location, time, direction, manner. Fixed, idiomatic → learn them: "a pain in the chest", not "at the chest".

  • Phrases: from diabetes, in the feet, of diabetic neuropathy, for three days, on his forearm.
  • Collocations: present with symptoms · suffer from a condition · respond to treatment · refer to a specialist · complain of pain.

8. Conjunctions p. 8–9

TypeFunctionExamples
CoordinatingConnect equal elementsand, but, or, so, for, nor, yet
SubordinatingConnect unequal elements (dependent → independent clause)because, although, since, while, when, if, unless
CorrelativePairs working togetherboth…and, either…or, neither…nor, not only…but also

"Although the patient is elderly, she remains mobile and independent." · "…discontinued because the patient developed adverse effects."

Watch out — common errors and PDF issues

Traps
  • Wrong word form from the same root: "The diagnose was confirmed" → diagnosis; "a diagnosis test" → diagnostic.
  • "a pain at the chest" → in the chest.
  • Functions words are a closed class — don't call prepositions "open".
  • The PDF's example "The rash appeared on his forearm since Monday" is itself faulty (since + past simple); "The rash appeared on his forearm on Monday" / "has been on his forearm since Monday" are correct. Its note on "allow" is also garbled — the correct pattern is allow + object + to-infinitive ("allow analysis to proceed").

Self-check — answer, then tap to reveal

Which 4 classes are content words and which 3 are function words?
Content: noun, verb, adjective, adverb. Function: pronoun, preposition, conjunction.
Why must prepositions be memorised but nouns can be predicted?
Function words are a closed class with fixed, idiomatic uses; content words follow word-formation patterns and context.
Give the verb, noun and adjective of the root "diagnos-".
diagnose · diagnosis · diagnostic.
Countable or uncountable: symptom, oxygen, blood, pain?
symptom countable; oxygen, blood, pain uncountable.
In "heart failure", what does "heart" do?
It's a noun used as an adjective, modifying "failure".
Which tense: "She has been prescribed metformin."?
Present perfect (passive).
Why can "gradually" vs "suddenly" matter clinically?
The manner and speed of onset can carry diagnostic significance.
Name the 4 correlative pairs.
both…and · either…or · neither…nor · not only…but also.
Label every word: "The experienced surgeon carefully examined the patient before surgery."
The (determiner/article) experienced (Adj) surgeon (N) carefully (Adv) examined (V) the (det.) patient (N) before (P) surgery (N).
