If you remember only this
  • Activity ai = γi · Ci/C₀ (dimensionless, C₀ = 1 mol/L). 0 < γ ≤ 1; γ = 1 for ideal (very dilute) solutions, pure substances and solvents.
  • Ionic strength I = ½ Σ Ck zk² (mol/L) — sum over every ion, with its own concentration and squared charge.
  • Debye–Hückel (I < 0.05 mol/L): log γi = −0.5 zi² √(I/I₀). Higher I → lower γ.
  • Chemical potential μi = μi⁰ + RT ln ai (J/mol). Water in a solution has a lower μ than pure water.
  • Osmolarity ω = [1 + α(β − 1)] C (osmol/L); β = ions per molecule, α = dissociation coefficient.

D2. Chemical activity slide 10 2024-25

Solute–solvent and solute–solute interactions reduce the solute's availability for reactions → active concentration (chemical activity):

ai = γi · CiC₀ ai dimensionless · C₀ = 1 mol/L (reference) · γi = activity coefficient, 0 < γi ≤ 1 · γi = 1 for ideal solutions, pure substances and solvents

D3. Ionic strength I slides 10–12 2022-23 · 2023-24 · 2024-25 · 2025-26

I = ½ Σk Ck zk²    [I] = mol/L k ions, each with concentration Ck and valence zk (zAl³⁺ = +3, zO²⁻ = −2, zH⁺ = +1)
log γi = −0.5 zi² √(I / I₀)    (Debye–Hückel, I < 0.05 mol/L) I₀ = 1 mol/L (reference ionic strength)
Exercise 3 (slides 11–12): Cl⁻ in MgCl₂ vs LaCl₃ at 10⁻³ mol/L MgCl₂ → Mg²⁺ (10⁻³) + 2Cl⁻ (2×10⁻³): I = ½[10⁻³×4 + 2×10⁻³×1] = 3 × 10⁻³ → γCl⁻ = 0.9389.
LaCl₃ → La³⁺ (10⁻³) + 3Cl⁻ (3×10⁻³): I = ½[10⁻³×9 + 3×10⁻³] = 6 × 10⁻³ → γCl⁻ = 0.9147.
Conclusion: I₁ < I₂ ⇒ γ₁ > γ₂ — the activity coefficient falls when ionic strength rises.
Worked: EMD1 2025-26 Q7 (key D = 0.84) 47.4 g KMnO₄ / 158 = 0.3 mol → K⁺ 0.3, MnO₄⁻ 0.3. 45 mL × 2 mol/L H₃PO₄ = 0.09 mol → H⁺ 0.27, PO₄³⁻ 0.09.
I = ½[0.3×1 + 0.3×1 + 0.27×1 + 0.09×9] = ½ × 1.68 = 0.84.
Memory trick Charges are squared: a 3⁻ ion counts 9 times, a 2⁺ ion 4 times. Write every ion's concentration first (number of ions × salt concentration), then square the charges.

D4. Chemical potential slides 13–15

μi(T) = μi⁰(T) + RT ln(ai)    [μ] = J/mol μi⁰ = potential in the standard state = the most stable physical state at Patm and T (water at 298 K: liquid)
  • The system's energy varies with the number of moles of species i → this gives i a potential μi.
  • Two phases at equilibrium: equal temperatures, pressures and chemical potentials.
  • Exercise 4.1: glucose 0.1 → 0.2 mmol/L at 27 °C (ideal): Δμ = RT ln(C₂/C₁) = 8.31 × 300 × ln 2 = 1730 J/mol.
  • Exercise 4.2: pure water = 55.5 mol/L; in a solution nwater < 55.5 → C₂ < C₁ (γ = 1 for both: pure body, solvent) → μ₂ − μ₁ = RT ln(C₂/C₁) < 0 → water's chemical potential is lower in solution than in pure water.

D5. Osmolarity of a solution slide 15 2022-23 · 2023-24 · 2024-25 · 2025-26

Exam traps

EMD1 2024-25 Q15 — only E was true
  • "The activity of a solution is its ability to dissociate" — false: activity = active concentration (γ·C/C₀).
  • "The activity coefficient is greater than 1" — false: 0 < γ ≤ 1.
  • "For a very dilute solution, the activity coefficient equals 1" — true (ideal solution).
  • Also offered: electric mobility statements (inversely proportional to the field, unit m²/s) — false, and not in these slides.
Ionic strength mistakes
  • Forgetting the ½, or forgetting to square z.
  • Using the salt concentration for every ion: in CaCl₂ at C, Cl⁻ is 2C; in Na₃PO₄, Na⁺ is 3C.
  • Volume of acid given in mL: moles = V (L) × C (e.g. 45 mL × 2 mol/L = 0.09 mol, in 1 L of solution).

Self-check — answer, then tap to reveal

Solution vs colloidal solution?
Solution: homogeneous, one phase. Colloidal: heterogeneous, > 1 phase, particles 10–1000 Å.
When is a solution considered ideal?
When very dilute, C ≤ 10⁻³ mol/L (interactions all about equal).
Range of γ and when it equals 1?
0 < γ ≤ 1; γ = 1 for ideal solutions, pure substances, solvents.
Ionic strength of 0.1 mol/L CaCl₂?
Ca²⁺ 0.1, Cl⁻ 0.2: I = ½(0.1×4 + 0.2×1) = 0.3 mol/L.
Debye–Hückel: formula and validity?
log γ = −0.5 z² √(I/I₀), for I < 0.05 mol/L.
Higher ionic strength → γ goes which way?
Down (I₁ < I₂ ⇒ γ₁ > γ₂).
Conditions of equilibrium between two phases?
Equal temperatures, pressures and chemical potentials.
Why is water's μ lower in a solution?
Its concentration is < 55.5 mol/L, so RT ln(C₂/C₁) < 0.
Osmolarity formula?
ω = [1 + α(β − 1)] C; non-electrolyte (β = 1): ω = C.
